Origins of Quantum Mechanics:
The Ultraviolet Catastrophe

Deep Synthesis • 4 min read

In the late 19th century, physicists believed they understood radiation. Classical physics, especially the work of Maxwell and Boltzmann, could successfully explain how light behaves at macroscopic scales. But when scientists turned their attention to the interaction between light and matter at the atomic scale, a troubling problem emerged.

According to classical theory, objects emit electromagnetic radiation at all wavelengths. As the wavelength becomes shorter (or the frequency higher), the theory predicted that the intensity of radiation should increase without bound.

“

The more we study nature, the more we stand amazed at the simplicity of her secrets.

– Max Planck
Nobel Laureate in Physics, 1918

This led to an absurd conclusion: a black body—a perfect emitter and absorber of radiation—should radiate infinite energy in the ultraviolet region. This contradiction between theory and experiment became known as the ultraviolet catastrophe.

The failure of classical physics at short wavelengths signaled the breakdown of an entire worldview and set the stage for a revolutionary idea: energy is quantized.
