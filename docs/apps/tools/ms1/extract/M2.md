## 3.13 Single Crystals

### Key ideas
- A single crystal is formed when the periodic and repeated atomic arrangement extends uninterrupted throughout the entire specimen.
- All unit cells within a single crystal interlock in an identical manner and possess the exact same geometric orientation.
- Single crystals occur in nature (e.g., mineral gemstones) but can also be synthesized artificially.
- Growing artificial single crystals is technically difficult because the growth environment (temperature gradients, cooling rates, purity) must be stringently controlled.
- When allowed to grow without external geometric constraints, a single crystal develops flat, planar faces forming a regular polyhedron whose external shape directly reflects its internal crystal structure.
- In modern technologies, single crystals are critical components—most notably electronic microcircuits, which rely on defect-controlled single crystals of silicon and other semiconductor materials.

### Definitions
- **single crystal**: A crystalline solid for which the periodic and repeated arrangement of atoms is perfect or extends throughout the entirety of the specimen without interruption, such that all unit cells interlock in the same way and possess the same orientation.

## 3.14 Polycrystalline Materials

### Key ideas
- The vast majority of engineering crystalline solids are polycrystalline, meaning they consist of an aggregate of many small crystals or grains.
- Solidification of a polycrystalline material proceeds through four distinct stages (represented in Figure 3.19):
  1. **Nucleation:** Small crystalline nuclei form independently at random locations throughout the liquid melt. Each nucleus possesses a random crystallographic orientation.
  2. **Growth:** Crystallites grow by capturing atoms from the surrounding liquid. Adjacent growing grains approach each other and begin to obstruct further lateral growth.
  3. **Impingement and completion:** The extremities of adjacent grains impinge upon one another as the liquid phase is completely exhausted. The resulting grains possess irregular boundaries conforming to the shapes dictated by mutual impingement, while preserving distinct crystallographic orientations.
  4. **Final grain structure:** Under microscopic observation after suitable polishing and etching, the structure displays grains separated by a boundary network.
- The narrow interfacial transition region where two misoriented grains meet is called a **grain boundary**. Within this boundary (a few atom diameters wide), atomic mismatch and irregular bonding exist.

### Definitions
- **grain**: An individual crystalline crystallite or small single crystal within a polycrystalline aggregate.
- **polycrystalline**: Crystalline materials composed of an assemblage of many small crystals or grains.
- **grain boundary**: The narrow interfacial boundary region separating two adjacent grains having different crystallographic orientations, characterized by atomic mismatch.

## 3.15 Anisotropy

### Key ideas
- The physical properties of single crystals often vary depending on the crystallographic direction along which the measurement is performed. This directional dependence is called **anisotropy**.
- Anisotropy arises because the interatomic spacing (and thus atomic packing density and bond strength) varies with crystallographic direction.
- Examples of anisotropic properties in single crystals include:
  - Modulus of elasticity ($E$)
  - Electrical conductivity ($\sigma$)
  - Index of refraction ($n$)
- Materials whose properties are completely independent of the direction of measurement are termed **isotropic**.
- The degree of anisotropy is governed by the structural symmetry of the crystal: anisotropy increases as structural symmetry decreases. For instance, low-symmetry triclinic crystals are typically highly anisotropic.
- In polycrystalline materials, if the crystallographic orientations of individual grains are randomly distributed, the directional variations of the grains average out. Consequently, the aggregate bulk specimen exhibits macroscopic isotropic behaviour, and measured properties represent directional averages.
- When grains in a polycrystalline material have a non-random, preferred crystallographic orientation, the material is said to possess a **texture**.
- Practical application of texture: Transformer cores utilize iron-silicon alloys engineered with a specific magnetic texture. Iron single crystals magnetize far more readily along $\langle 100 \rangle$-type directions than along any other direction. By rolling and heat-treating sheets so that most grains have a $\langle 100 \rangle$ axis aligned parallel to the applied magnetic field, electrical energy losses due to core hysteresis are minimized.

### Definitions
- **anisotropy**: The phenomenon whereby the physical properties of a material depend on the crystallographic direction in which measurements are taken; associated with the variance of atomic or ionic spacing with crystallographic direction.
- **isotropic**: Possessing physical properties that are identical in all directions of measurement.
- **texture**: A preferred crystallographic orientation exhibited by the grains in a polycrystalline material.

## 3.16 X-Ray Diffraction: Determination of Crystal Structures

### Key ideas
- **Diffraction conditions:** Diffraction occurs when a wave encounters regularly spaced obstacles that:
  1. Are capable of scattering the wave.
  2. Possess spacings comparable in magnitude to the wavelength of the radiation.
- Diffraction is a consequence of definite phase relationships established between scattered waves.
  - **Constructive interference:** Occurs when scattered waves traverse path lengths that differ by an integer number of wavelengths ($m\lambda$). The waves remain in phase, and their amplitudes add together ($A + A = 2A$), yielding a reinforced diffracted beam.
  - **Destructive interference:** Occurs when scattered waves traverse path lengths differing by an odd integral number of half-wavelengths ($[m + 1/2]\lambda$). The waves are exactly $180^\circ$ out of phase, their amplitudes cancel, and the resultant wave amplitude is zero.
- **X-rays:** Electromagnetic radiation having high energies and short wavelengths on the order of atomic interplanar spacings in solids ($\sim 0.1\text{ nm}$).
- **X-ray scattering mechanism:** X-rays are scattered in all directions by the electrons surrounding each atom or ion in the crystal.
- **Bragg's Law:** Parallel lattice planes with Miller indices $(hkl)$ separated by interplanar distance $d_{hkl}$ scatter an incident monochromatic, coherent X-ray beam of wavelength $\lambda$ incident at angle $\theta$. Constructive interference between rays scattered by adjacent planes requires the extra path length $(SQ + QT = 2d_{hkl}\sin\theta)$ to equal an integral number $n$ of wavelengths:
  $$n\lambda = 2d_{hkl}\sin\theta$$
- **Necessary vs. sufficient condition:** Bragg's law is a necessary condition for diffraction, but not a sufficient one for real crystals. Bragg's law assumes scattering centers are positioned only at unit cell corners. Atoms residing at internal or face sites (such as in BCC and FCC) act as additional scattering centers that can cause out-of-phase destructive scattering at certain Bragg angles, completely extinguishing particular diffraction peaks.
- **Reflection rules (selection rules):**
  - For **BCC**: Diffraction occurs only for planes where $(h + k + l)$ is an **even** integer (e.g., $110, 200, 211, 220, 310, 222$). (Note: $0$ is considered an even integer).
  - For **FCC**: Diffraction occurs only for planes where $h, k,$ and $l$ are **either all odd or all even** (e.g., $111, 200, 220, 311, 222, 400$).
  - For **Simple Cubic**: All sets of crystallographic planes produce diffracted beams (no systematic absences).
- **Diffractometer operation:**
  - A flat-plate powder specimen ($S$) contains millions of randomly oriented crystalline particles/grains, ensuring every possible crystallographic plane satisfies the Bragg orientation condition.
  - The specimen rotates about axis $O$ by angle $\theta$, while an X-ray detector/counter carriage ($C$) rotates synchronously about the same axis by angle $2\theta$.
  - This $1:2$ mechanical coupling maintains the angle of incidence equal to the angle of reflection.
  - Collimators produce a focused, parallel beam, and filters produce a monochromatic beam.
  - A detector measures diffracted beam intensity as a function of the diffraction angle ($2\theta$).
- **Primary uses of X-ray diffractometry:**
  1. Determination of crystal structure: peak positions ($2\theta$) determine unit cell size, geometry, and lattice parameters; relative peak intensities determine atomic positions inside the unit cell.
  2. Determination of crystallographic orientations of single crystals using Laue back-reflection or transmission photography.
  3. Qualitative and quantitative chemical phase identification.
  4. Measurement of residual internal stresses.
  5. Measurement of crystallite/grain size.

### Definitions
- **diffraction**: The phenomenon whereby a wave encounters a series of regularly spaced obstacles that scatter the wave, resulting in the mutual reinforcement (constructive interference) of scattered waves that satisfy specific phase relationships.
- **Bragg's law**: The mathematical condition for constructive diffraction of X-rays by parallel planes of atoms: $n\lambda = 2d_{hkl}\sin\theta$.
- **order of reflection ($n$)**: An integer ($1, 2, 3, \dots$) representing the number of whole wavelengths in the path-length difference between waves scattered from adjacent atomic planes.
- **interplanar spacing ($d_{hkl}$)**: The perpendicular distance separating adjacent, parallel crystallographic planes having Miller indices $h, k,$ and $l$.
- **diffraction angle ($2\theta$)**: The experimentally measured angle between the undeflected transmitted beam and the diffracted X-ray beam.
- **reflection rules**: Crystallographic conditions dictating which sets of Miller indices $(hkl)$ yield constructive diffraction beams and which produce zero intensity due to destructive phase cancellation by non-corner atoms.

### Equations
- **Equation (3.19):** Path length difference condition for diffraction
  $$n\lambda = SQ + QT$$
  - $n$: order of reflection ($1, 2, 3, \dots$, unitless)
  - $\lambda$: wavelength of X-ray radiation (m or nm)
  - $SQ, QT$: geometric path segments traversed by scattered ray ($SQ = QT = d_{hkl}\sin\theta$) (m or nm)
- **Equation (3.20):** Bragg's Law
  $$n\lambda = 2d_{hkl}\sin\theta$$
  - $n$: order of reflection (integer $1, 2, 3, \dots$; must satisfy $\sin\theta \le 1$)
  - $\lambda$: X-ray wavelength (nm or m)
  - $d_{hkl}$: interplanar spacing of diffracting planes $(hkl)$ (nm or m)
  - $\theta$: Bragg angle / angle of incidence and reflection relative to the crystallographic plane (degrees or radians)
- **Equation (3.21):** Interplanar spacing for cubic crystal systems
  $$d_{hkl} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{h^2 + k^2 + l^2}}$$
  - $d_{hkl}$: interplanar spacing of planes with indices $h, k, l$ (nm or m)
  - $a$: cubic lattice parameter / unit cell edge length (nm or m)
  - $h, k, l$: Miller indices of the diffracting crystallographic planes (integers)

### Worked examples
- **Example Problem 3.13: Interplanar Spacing and Diffraction Angle Computations**
  - *Problem Statement:* "For BCC iron, compute (a) the interplanar spacing and (b) the diffraction angle for the (220) set of planes. The lattice parameter for Fe is 0.2866 nm. Assume that monochromatic radiation having a wavelength of 0.1790 nm is used, and the order of reflection is 1."
  - *Solution:*
    - **(a)** The interplanar spacing $d_{hkl}$ is calculated using Equation 3.21 with $a = 0.2866\text{ nm}$, $h = 2$, $k = 2$, and $l = 0$:
      $$d_{220} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{h^2 + k^2 + l^2}} = \frac{0.2866\text{ nm}}{\sqrt{(2)^2 + (2)^2 + (0)^2}} = \frac{0.2866\text{ nm}}{\sqrt{8}} = 0.1013\text{ nm}$$
    - **(b)** The value of $\theta$ is determined from Bragg's law (Equation 3.20) with $n = 1$:
      $$\sin\theta = \frac{n\lambda}{2d_{220}} = \frac{(1)(0.1790\text{ nm})}{(2)(0.1013\text{ nm})} = 0.884$$
      $$\theta = \sin^{-1}(0.884) = 62.13^\circ$$
      The diffraction angle is $2\theta$:
      $$2\theta = 2(62.13^\circ) = 124.26^\circ$$

- **Example Problem 3.14: Interplanar Spacing and Lattice Parameter Computations for Lead**
  - *Problem Statement:* "Figure 3.23 shows an x-ray diffraction pattern for lead taken using a diffractometer and monochromatic x-radiation having a wavelength of 0.1542 nm; each diffraction peak on the pattern has been indexed. Compute the interplanar spacing for each set of planes indexed; also, determine the lattice parameter of Pb for each of the peaks. For all peaks, assume the order of diffraction is 1."
  - *Solution:*
    - For each indexed peak, interplanar spacing $d_{hkl}$ is computed using Bragg's law:
      $$d_{hkl} = \frac{n\lambda}{2\sin(2\theta / 2)}$$

      $$a = d_{hkl}\sqrt{h^2 + k^2 + l^2}$$

      $$d_{111} = \frac{(1)(0.1542\text{ nm})}{2\sin(31.3^\circ / 2)} = \frac{0.1542}{2\sin(15.65^\circ)} = 0.2858\text{ nm}$$
      $$a = d_{111}\sqrt{(1)^2 + (1)^2 + (1)^2} = (0.2858\text{ nm})\sqrt{3} = 0.4950\text{ nm}$$
    - *Calculations for remaining four peaks:*
      - For $(200)$ at $2\theta = 36.6^\circ$:

### Concept checks
- **Concept Check 3.3**
  - *Question verbatim:* "For cubic crystals, as values of the planar indices h, k, and l increase, does the distance between adjacent and parallel planes (i.e., the interplanar spacing) increase or decrease? Why?"
  - *Answer:* The interplanar spacing $d_{hkl}$ decreases. According to Equation 3.21 ($d_{hkl} = a/\sqrt{h^2 + k^2 + l^2}$), the lattice parameter $a$ is constant for a given crystal; hence, as the Miller indices $h, k,$ and $l$ increase, the denominator increases, which directly causes the value of $d_{hkl}$ to decrease.

## 3.17 Noncrystalline Solids

### Key ideas
- Noncrystalline solids lack a systematic, regular arrangement of atoms or ions over relatively large atomic distances (they lack long-range order).
- They are commonly referred to as **amorphous** ("without form") or **supercooled liquids**, because their atomic arrangements closely resemble the disordered state of a liquid.
- Comparison of crystalline and noncrystalline structures is illustrated by silicon dioxide ($\text{SiO}_2$):
  - In both crystalline and amorphous $\text{SiO}_2$, basic short-range bonding is identical: each silicon atom is bonded to three oxygen atoms in-plane (and a fourth oxygen atom positioned above the plane), forming $\text{SiO}_4^{4-}$ tetrahedra.
  - In crystalline silica (quartz), these tetrahedra form a regular, periodic array of uniform hexagonal rings.
  - In noncrystalline silica (silica glass), the tetrahedra connect at variable bond angles to form an irregular, disordered network of variable-sized rings.
- **Factors determining crystalline vs. amorphous solid formation:**
  1. *Structural complexity:* Materials with complex atomic or molecular structures become ordered with great difficulty during solidification, favoring an amorphous solid.
  2. *Cooling rate:* Rapid cooling (quenching) through the freezing temperature strongly favors noncrystalline formation because insufficient time is allowed for mobile atoms to organize into periodic lattice positions.
- **Class behavior:**
  - *Metals:* Almost always form crystalline solids because metallic bonding is non-directional and atomic unit cells are dense and simple.
  - *Ceramics:* Some are crystalline; others—specifically inorganic oxide glasses—are amorphous.
  - *Polymers:* May be completely noncrystalline (amorphous) or semicrystalline (having localized crystalline domains embedded within amorphous regions).

### Definitions
- **noncrystalline**: Solids that lack a systematic and regular arrangement of atoms over relatively large atomic distances on an atomic scale.
- **amorphous**: Noncrystalline; literally meaning "without form"; having a disordered atomic structure resembling that of a liquid.
- **supercooled liquids**: Another designation for amorphous or noncrystalline solids, highlighting their structural resemblance to a frozen liquid state.

## 4.1 Introduction

### Key ideas
- Ideal crystalline solids with perfect atomic order do not exist in reality; all engineering materials contain large quantities of various defects or imperfections.
- Deviations from crystalline perfection strongly dictate material properties. Imperfections are not always detrimental; specific engineering characteristics are deliberately created by adding controlled quantities of specific defects (e.g., solid-solution strengthening, semiconductor doping).
- A **crystalline defect** is defined as a lattice irregularity having one or more dimensions on the order of an atomic diameter.
- Classification of imperfections is based on defect geometry or dimensionality:
  1. **Point defects:** Zero-dimensional (0D) imperfections associated with one or two atomic positions (e.g., vacancies, self-interstitials, substitutional and interstitial impurity atoms).
  2. **Linear defects:** One-dimensional (1D) imperfections known as dislocations.
  3. **Interfacial defects (boundaries):** Two-dimensional (2D) planar boundaries separating regions with different crystal structures or crystallographic orientations.
  4. **Bulk or volume defects:** Three-dimensional (3D) macroscopic or microscopic defects (pores, cracks, inclusions).
- Chapter 4 also covers techniques for the microscopic characterization of defects and microstructure.

### Definitions
- **imperfection**: Any deviation in a solid material from ideal crystalline atomic order and perfection.
- **crystalline defect**: A lattice irregularity having one or more of its dimensions on the order of an atomic diameter.
- **point defect**: A crystalline defect associated with one or two atomic positions (zero-dimensional).

## 4.2 Vacancies and Self-Interstitials

### Key ideas
- A **vacancy** (vacant lattice site) is the simplest point defect: an atom site normally occupied in the perfect crystal lattice from which an atom is missing.
- All crystalline materials intrinsically contain vacancies. It is thermodynamically impossible to produce a crystal free of vacancies because the introduction of vacancies increases the configurational entropy (randomness) of the crystal.
- The equilibrium vacancy concentration increases exponentially with absolute temperature according to an Arrhenius-type relationship ($N_v = N \exp[-Q_v/kT]$).
- For most metals, the vacancy fraction $N_v / N$ just below the melting temperature is on the order of $10^{-4}$ (approximately $1$ vacant site per $10,000$ lattice sites).
- A **self-interstitial** is a host crystal atom that has been crowded into an interstitial site—a small void space between host atoms that is normally unoccupied.
- In metals, a self-interstitial causes severe, large compressive lattice distortions in the surrounding lattice because the host atom is substantially larger than the interstitial void into which it is forced.
- Consequently, the formation energy of a self-interstitial is much higher than that of a vacancy, its formation probability is very low, and its equilibrium concentration in metals is negligibly small compared to vacancy concentrations.

### Definitions
- **vacancy**: A point defect representing a vacant or unoccupied lattice site in a crystal that is normally occupied by an atom.
- **Boltzmann's constant ($k$)**: A fundamental constant of nature relating thermal energy to temperature, with values $1.38 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J/atom}\cdot\text{K}$ or $8.62 \times 10^{-5}\text{ eV/atom}\cdot\text{K}$.
- **self-interstitial**: A point defect created when an atom from the host crystal is crowded into an interstitial void space between regular lattice sites.

### Equations
- **Equation (4.1):** Temperature dependence of equilibrium number of vacancies
  $$N_v = N \exp\left(-\frac{Q_v}{kT}\right)$$
  - $N_v$: equilibrium number of vacancies per unit volume (usually $\text{m}^{-3}$)
  - $N$: total number of atomic lattice sites per unit volume ($\text{m}^{-3}$)
  - $Q_v$: energy required to form a single vacancy ($\text{J/mol}$ or $\text{eV/atom}$)
  - $T$: absolute temperature in kelvins ($\text{K} = ^\circ\text{C} + 273$)
  - $k$: Boltzmann's constant ($1.38 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J/atom}\cdot\text{K}$ or $8.62 \times 10^{-5}\text{ eV/atom}\cdot\text{K}$)
  - *Note:* When $Q_v$ is expressed in $\text{J/mol}$, $k$ is replaced by the universal gas constant $R = 8.31\text{ J/mol}\cdot\text{K}$.
- **Equation (4.2):** Number of atomic sites per unit volume for a metal
  $$N = \frac{N_A \rho}{A}$$
  - $N$: total number of atomic sites per unit volume ($\text{atoms/m}^3$ or $\text{atoms/cm}^3$)
  - $N_A$: Avogadro's number ($6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ atoms/mol}$)
  - $\rho$: material density ($\text{g/cm}^3$ or $\text{kg/m}^3$)
  - $A$: atomic weight ($\text{g/mol}$)

### Numbers & tables
- Boltzmann's constant:
  - $k = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J/atom}\cdot\text{K}$
  - $k = 8.62 \times 10^{-5}\text{ eV/atom}\cdot\text{K}$
- Universal gas constant: $R = 8.31\text{ J/mol}\cdot\text{K}$
- Avogadro's number: $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ atoms/mol}$
- Vacancy fraction just below melting point: $N_v / N \approx 10^{-4}$ ($1$ in $10,000$)
- In Example 4.1:
  - Copper vacancy formation energy: $Q_v = 0.9\text{ eV/atom}$
  - Temperature: $1000^\circ\text{C} = 1273\text{ K}$
  - Atomic weight of copper: $A_{\text{Cu}} = 63.5\text{ g/mol}$
  - Density of copper at $1000^\circ\text{C}$: $\rho = 8.4\text{ g/cm}^3$
  - Atomic site concentration: $N = 8.0 \times 10^{28}\text{ atoms/m}^3$
  - Vacancy concentration: $N_v = 2.2 \times 10^{25}\text{ vacancies/m}^3$

### Worked examples
- **Example Problem 4.1: Number-of-Vacancies Computation at a Specified Temperature**
  - *Problem Statement:* "Calculate the equilibrium number of vacancies per cubic meter for copper at 1000°C. The energy for vacancy formation is 0.9 eV/atom; the atomic weight and density (at 1000°C) for copper are 63.5 g/mol and 8.4 g/cm3, respectively."
  - *Solution:*
    - First compute the total number of atomic sites per cubic meter $N_{\text{Cu}}$ using Equation 4.2:
      $$N_{\text{Cu}} = \frac{N_A \rho}{A_{\text{Cu}}} = \frac{(6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ atoms/mol})(8.4\text{ g/cm}^3)(10^6\text{ cm}^3/\text{m}^3)}{63.5\text{ g/mol}} = 8.0 \times 10^{28}\text{ atoms/m}^3$$

      $$T = 1000^\circ\text{C} + 273 = 1273\text{ K}$$
    - Calculate equilibrium vacancy concentration using Equation 4.1:
      $$N_v = N \exp\left(-\frac{Q_v}{kT}\right)$$
      $$N_v = (8.0 \times 10^{28}\text{ atoms/m}^3) \exp\left[-\frac{0.9\text{ eV}}{(8.62 \times 10^{-5}\text{ eV/atom}\cdot\text{K})(1273\text{ K})}\right]$$
      $$N_v = (8.0 \times 10^{28}) \exp(-8.204) = 2.2 \times 10^{25}\text{ vacancies/m}^3$$

## 4.3 Impurities in Solids

### Key ideas
- Pure metals containing only one type of atom cannot be synthesized. Even with advanced refining techniques, the maximum achievable purity is rarely above $99.9999\%$. At this purity, $10^{22}$ to $10^{23}$ impurity atoms still reside in every cubic meter of material.
- Most engineering metals are **alloys**, in which solute impurity atoms are intentionally introduced to enhance properties such as mechanical strength, hardness, and corrosion resistance.
  - *Example:* Sterling silver is an alloy of $92.5\text{ wt}\%\text{ Ag}$ and $7.5\text{ wt}\%\text{ Cu}$. Pure silver is ductile and soft; adding copper increases mechanical strength while preserving ambient corrosion resistance.
- Adding impurity atoms creates a **solid solution** and/or a separate second phase.
- In a solid solution, solute atoms dissolve in the solvent without altering the solvent's crystal structure, producing a compositionally homogeneous single phase.
  - **Solvent (host atoms):** Component present in greatest concentration.
  - **Solute:** Component present in minor concentration.
- **Two types of solid solutions:**
  1. **Substitutional solid solutions:** Solute atoms occupy normal lattice positions by replacing/substituting for host solvent atoms (Figure 4.2).
  2. **Interstitial solid solutions:** Solute atoms locate within the small void spaces (interstices) between host atoms.
- **The Hume-Rothery Rules (substitutional solid solubility):**
  High mutual solubility in substitutional systems occurs when four empirical conditions are met:
  1. *Atomic size factor:* The difference in atomic radii ($\Delta r$) between solute and solvent must be less than approximately $\pm 15\%$. If $|\Delta r| > 15\%$, substantial lattice strain develops and a new phase forms.
  2. *Crystal structure:* Both metal components must share the same crystal structure.
  3. *Electronegativity factor:* The electronegativities of both elements must be relatively close. If one is strongly electropositive and the other strongly electronegative, they tend to form an intermetallic compound rather than an extensive solid solution.
  4. *Valences:* A metal tends to dissolve another metal of higher valency more readily than one of lower valency.
  - *Example:* The Copper–Nickel ($\text{Cu}$–$\text{Ni}$) system forms a complete substitutional solid solution across all proportions ($0$ to $100\%$):
    - $r_{\text{Cu}} = 0.128\text{ nm}, r_{\text{Ni}} = 0.125\text{ nm}$ (size difference is only $2.3\%$, well within $15\%$).
    - Both possess FCC crystal structures.
    - Electronegativities are nearly identical ($1.9$ for $\text{Cu}$, $1.8$ for $\text{Ni}$).
    - Valences are $+1$ ($+2$) for $\text{Cu}$ and $+2$ for $\text{Ni}$.
- **Interstitial solid solution characteristics:**
  - In FCC and BCC unit cells, two types of interstitial sites exist:
    - **Tetrahedral sites:** Coordination number is $4$. Connecting host atom centers produces a four-sided regular tetrahedron.
    - **Octahedral sites:** Coordination number is $6$. Connecting host atom centers produces an eight-sided regular octahedron.
  - **Interstitial site coordinates in FCC:**
    - Octahedral sites: two equivalent locations: unit cell edge centers (e.g., $0\,\frac{1}{2}\,1$) and the body center ($\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{1}{2}$).
    - Tetrahedral sites: located at internal quarter-coordinates (e.g., $\frac{1}{4}\,\frac{3}{4}\,\frac{1}{4}$).
  - **Interstitial site coordinates in BCC:**
    - Octahedral sites: face centers (e.g., $\frac{1}{2}\,1\,\frac{1}{2}$) and edge centers (e.g., $\frac{1}{2}\,1\,0$).
    - Tetrahedral sites: face locations (e.g., $1\,\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{1}{4}$).
  - Because metals have high atomic packing factors, interstitial void spaces are very small. Consequently, interstitial solute atoms must have diameters substantially smaller than host atoms.
  - The maximum allowable concentration of interstitial atoms is generally low (typically $< 10\%$).
  - Even small solute atoms are slightly larger than the interstitial voids they occupy, introducing localized lattice strains on adjacent host atoms.
  - *Example:* Carbon in iron. The atomic radius of carbon is $0.071\text{ nm}$, compared to $0.124\text{ nm}$ for iron. The maximum solubility of carbon in $\alpha$-iron is approximately $2\text{ wt}\%$ (achieved at elevated temperature in FCC $\gamma$-iron; room-temperature solubility in BCC $\alpha$-ferrite is much lower).
  - In Example Problem 4.2, the radius $r$ of an impurity atom fitting into a BCC octahedral site without strain is calculated as $r = 0.155R$.

### Definitions
- **alloy**: A metallic material composed of two or more elements.
- **solid solution**: A homogeneous crystalline phase containing two or more chemical species where the host crystal structure is maintained and no new phases are formed.
- **solute**: The chemical component in a solution present in a minor concentration.
- **solvent**: The chemical component in a solution present in the greatest concentration (also termed host atoms).
- **substitutional solid solution**: A solid solution in which solute atoms replace or substitute for host solvent atoms on normal lattice positions.
- **interstitial solid solution**: A solid solution in which solute atoms occupy interstitial sites (void spaces) between the host atoms.
- **Hume-Rothery rules**: Four empirical criteria (atomic size difference $< \pm 15\%$, identical crystal structure, similar electronegativities, and valence compatibility) governing extensive substitutional solid solubility in metals.

### Equations
- Interstitial radius derivation in BCC octahedral site (Example 4.2):
  $$\text{Unit cell edge length } a = \frac{4R}{\sqrt{3}} = 2R + 2r$$
  $$2r = \frac{4R}{\sqrt{3}} - 2R = 2\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} - 1\right)R$$
  $$r = \left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} - 1\right)R = 0.155R$$
  - $r$: radius of unstrained interstitial impurity atom (nm or m)
  - $R$: radius of host metal atom (nm or m)

### Numbers & tables
- Purity threshold: refining metals above $99.9999\%$ still leaves $10^{22}$–$10^{23}$ impurity atoms/$\text{m}^3$.
- Sterling silver composition: $92.5\text{ wt}\%\text{ Ag}$, $7.5\text{ wt}\%\text{ Cu}$.
- Hume-Rothery atomic size limit: $|\Delta r| < \pm 15\%$.
- Copper–Nickel parameters:
  - $r_{\text{Cu}} = 0.128\text{ nm}$, $r_{\text{Ni}} = 0.125\text{ nm}$
  - Crystal structure: FCC for both
  - Electronegativity: $1.9$ for $\text{Cu}$, $1.8$ for $\text{Ni}$
  - Common valences: $+1$ (sometimes $+2$) for $\text{Cu}$, $+2$ for $\text{Ni}$

  - Carbon atomic radius: $r_{\text{C}} = 0.071\text{ nm}$
  - Iron atomic radius: $R_{\text{Fe}} = 0.124\text{ nm}$
  - Maximum carbon concentration in iron solid solution: $\approx 2\%$.
  - Interstitial solid solutions maximum solubility: generally $< 10\%$.

### Worked examples
- **Example Problem 4.2: Computation of Radius of BCC Interstitial Site**
  - *Problem Statement:* "Compute the radius r of an impurity atom that just fits into a BCC octahedral site in terms of the atomic radius R of the host atom (without introducing lattice strains)."
  - *Solution:*
    - In BCC, an octahedral interstitial site is situated at the center of a unit cell edge (Figure 4.3b).
    - For the interstitial atom to fit without introducing strain, it must just touch the two corner host atoms along the cube edge.
    - From the geometry of the BCC unit cell (Equation 3.4):
      $$\text{Unit cell edge length } a = \frac{4R}{\sqrt{3}}$$
    - From the physical contact of the three atoms along the cube edge:
      $$\text{Unit cell edge length } a = 2R + 2r$$
    - Equating these two expressions:
      $$2R + 2r = \frac{4R}{\sqrt{3}}$$
    - Solving for $r$ in terms of $R$:
      $$2r = \frac{4R}{\sqrt{3}} - 2R = 2\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} - 1\right)R$$
      $$r = \left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} - 1\right)R = (1.1547 - 1)R = 0.155R$$

### Concept checks
- **Concept Check 4.1**
  - *Question verbatim:* "Is it possible for three or more elements to form a solid solution? Explain your answer."
  - *Answer:* Yes, it is entirely possible for three or more elements to form a solid solution. Multiple solute species can dissolve simultaneously into a host solvent metal—either substitutionally (if Hume-Rothery criteria are satisfied) or interstitially (if solute atoms are sufficiently small). For example, commercial alloy steels represent solid solutions where iron acts as the solvent host, carbon dissolves interstitially, and elements such as nickel, chromium, and manganese dissolve substitutionally.
- **Concept Check 4.2**
  - *Question verbatim:* "Explain why complete solid solubility may occur for substitutional solid solutions but not for interstitial solid solutions."
  - *Answer:* Complete solid solubility requires that solute and solvent atoms be completely interchangeable across the entire composition spectrum from $0\%$ to $100\%$ without precipitating a new phase or changing the basic crystal structure. This is achievable in substitutional systems where both atoms have the same crystal structure and similar sizes (e.g., Cu–Ni). In interstitial solid solutions, solute atoms must occupy the interstitial void spaces of the host crystal. Because interstitial spaces are finite in number and substantially smaller than host atoms, high interstitial solute concentrations induce massive lattice strain, inevitably causing the formation of a distinct new compound or second phase long before reaching $100\%$ solute content.

## 4.4 Specification of Composition

### Key ideas
- Alloy composition (or concentration) defines the relative content of each constituent element.
- The two primary conventions for expressing composition are:
  1. **Weight percent ($\text{wt}\%$):** Based on the mass of a specific element divided by the total alloy mass.
  2. **Atom percent ($\text{at}\%$):** Based on the number of moles (or atoms) of an element divided by the total moles (or atoms) in the alloy.
- Analytical conversions between weight percent and atom percent allow transition between bulk mass measurements and atomic/lattice calculations.
- Additional composition bases include mass of an element per unit volume of alloy ($\text{kg/m}^3$), which is frequently utilized in diffusion equations.
- Density ($\rho_{\text{ave}}$) and average atomic weight ($A_{\text{ave}}$) of binary alloys can be estimated from weight percent or atom percent.
- *Volume additivity assumption:* The derivations of mass per unit volume (Equations 4.9) and average density/atomic weight (Equations 4.10, 4.11) assume that the total alloy volume equals the sum of the unmixed volumes of the individual components. While not rigorously exact for all real alloys, this assumption is highly accurate for dilute solutions and across typical solid-solution composition ranges.

### Definitions
- **composition**: The relative content of a specific element or constituent in an alloy (used interchangeably with concentration).
- **weight percent (wt%)**: The weight (or mass) of a particular element relative to the total alloy weight, multiplied by 100.
- **atom percent (at%)**: The number of moles (or atoms) of a particular element relative to the total number of moles (or atoms) of all elements in the alloy, multiplied by 100.

### Equations
- **Equation (4.3a):** Weight percent of component 1 in a two-element alloy
  $$C_1 = \frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2} \times 100$$
  - $C_1$: weight percent of element 1 ($\text{wt}\%$)
  - $m_1, m_2$: mass (or weight) of elements 1 and 2 (g or kg)
- **Equation (4.3b):** Weight percent for multi-component alloys ($n$ elements)
  $$C_1 = \frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2 + m_3 + \dots + m_n} \times 100$$
- **Equation (4.4):** Number of moles in mass $m'_1$ of element 1
  $$n_{m1} = \frac{m'_1}{A_1}$$
  - $n_{m1}$: number of moles of element 1 ($\text{mol}$)
  - $m'_1$: mass of element 1 in grams ($\text{g}$)
  - $A_1$: atomic weight of element 1 ($\text{g/mol}$)
- **Equation (4.5a):** Atom percent of component 1 in a two-element alloy
  $$C'_1 = \frac{n_{m1}}{n_{m1} + n_{m2}} \times 100$$
  - $C'_1$: atom percent of element 1 ($\text{at}\%$)
  - $n_{m1}, n_{m2}$: number of moles of elements 1 and 2 ($\text{mol}$)
- **Equation (4.5b):** Atom percent for multi-component alloys ($n$ elements)
  $$C'_1 = \frac{n_{m1}}{n_{m1} + n_{m2} + n_{m3} + \dots + n_{mn}} \times 100$$
- **Equations (4.6a, 4.6b):** Conversion from weight percent to atom percent (binary alloy)
  $$C'_1 = \frac{C_1 A_2}{C_1 A_2 + C_2 A_1} \times 100$$
  $$C'_2 = \frac{C_2 A_1}{C_1 A_2 + C_2 A_1} \times 100$$
  - $C'_1, C'_2$: compositions in atom percent ($\text{at}\%$)
  - $C_1, C_2$: compositions in weight percent ($\text{wt}\%$)
  - $A_1, A_2$: atomic weights of elements 1 and 2 ($\text{g/mol}$)
- **Equations (4.7a, 4.7b):** Conversion from atom percent to weight percent (binary alloy)
  $$C_1 = \frac{C'_1 A_1}{C'_1 A_1 + C'_2 A_2} \times 100$$
  $$C_2 = \frac{C'_2 A_2}{C'_1 A_1 + C'_2 A_2} \times 100$$
- **Equations (4.8a, 4.8b):** Binary sum identities
  $$C_1 + C_2 = 100$$
  $$C'_1 + C'_2 = 100$$
- **Equations (4.9a, 4.9b):** Conversion of weight percent to mass per unit volume ($C''_1, C''_2$)
  $$C''_1 = \frac{C_1}{\left(\frac{C_1}{\rho_1} + \frac{C_2}{\rho_2}\right)} \times 10^3$$
  $$C''_2 = \frac{C_2}{\left(\frac{C_1}{\rho_1} + \frac{C_2}{\rho_2}\right)} \times 10^3$$
  - $C''_1, C''_2$: concentrations in mass per unit volume ($\text{kg/m}^3$)
  - $\rho_1, \rho_2$: individual elemental densities in $\text{g/cm}^3$ (the factor $10^3$ converts $\text{g/cm}^3$ to $\text{kg/m}^3$)
- **Equations (4.10a, 4.10b):** Average density of a binary alloy ($\rho_{\text{ave}}$)
  - From weight percent:
    $$\rho_{\text{ave}} = \frac{100}{\frac{C_1}{\rho_1} + \frac{C_2}{\rho_2}}$$
  - From atom percent:
    $$\rho_{\text{ave}} = \frac{C'_1 A_1 + C'_2 A_2}{\frac{C'_1 A_1}{\rho_1} + \frac{C'_2 A_2}{\rho_2}}$$
- **Equations (4.11a, 4.11b):** Average atomic weight of a binary alloy ($A_{\text{ave}}$)
  - From weight percent:
    $$A_{\text{ave}} = \frac{100}{\frac{C_1}{A_1} + \frac{C_2}{A_2}}$$
  - From atom percent:
    $$A_{\text{ave}} = \frac{C'_1 A_1 + C'_2 A_2}{100}$$

- Conversion factor: $10^3$ in Equation 4.9 converts $\text{g/cm}^3$ to $\text{kg/m}^3$ when combined with percentage units.

  - Copper concentration: $C_{\text{Cu}} = 3\text{ wt}\%$

- **Example Problem 4.3: Derivation of Composition-Conversion Equation**
  - *Problem Statement:* "Derive Equation 4.6a."
  - *Solution:*
    - Let masses be expressed in grams and denoted with a prime ($m'_1, m'_2$). Total alloy mass $M'$ is:
      $$M' = m'_1 + m'_2 \quad (4.12)$$
    - From the definition of $C'_1$ (Equation 4.5a) and the mole definition $n_{m1} = m'_1/A_1$:
      $$C'_1 = \frac{n_{m1}}{n_{m1} + n_{m2}} \times 100 = \frac{\frac{m'_1}{A_1}}{\frac{m'_1}{A_1} + \frac{m'_2}{A_2}} \times 100 \quad (4.13)$$
    - Rearranging Equation 4.3a gives the mass in grams in terms of weight percent $C_1$ and total mass $M'$:
      $$m'_1 = \frac{C_1 M'}{100} \quad (4.14)$$
      $$m'_2 = \frac{C_2 M'}{100}$$
    - Substituting these mass expressions into Equation 4.13 yields:
      $$C'_1 = \frac{\frac{C_1 M'}{100 A_1}}{\frac{C_1 M'}{100 A_1} + \frac{C_2 M'}{100 A_2}} \times 100 \quad (4.15)$$
    - Factoring out and canceling the common factor $M'/100$:
      $$C'_1 = \frac{\frac{C_1}{A_1}}{\frac{C_1}{A_1} + \frac{C_2}{A_2}} \times 100$$
    - Multiplying numerator and denominator by $A_1 A_2$:
      $$C'_1 = \frac{C_1 A_2}{C_1 A_2 + C_2 A_1} \times 100$$
      which is identical to Equation 4.6a.

- **Example Problem 4.4: Composition Conversion—From Weight Percent to Atom Percent**
  - *Problem Statement:* "Determine the composition, in atom percent, of an alloy that consists of 97 wt% aluminum and 3 wt% copper."
  - *Solution:*
    - Given: $C_{\text{Al}} = 97$, $C_{\text{Cu}} = 3$, $A_{\text{Al}} = 26.98\text{ g/mol}$, $A_{\text{Cu}} = 63.55\text{ g/mol}$.

      $$C'_{\text{Al}} = \frac{C_{\text{Al}} A_{\text{Cu}}}{C_{\text{Al}} A_{\text{Cu}} + C_{\text{Cu}} A_{\text{Al}}} \times 100$$
      $$C'_{\text{Al}} = \frac{(97)(63.55\text{ g/mol})}{(97)(63.55\text{ g/mol}) + (3)(26.98\text{ g/mol})} \times 100 = \frac{6164.35}{6164.35 + 80.94} \times 100 = 98.7\text{ at}\%$$

## 4.5 Dislocations—Linear Defects

### Key ideas
- A **dislocation** is a linear or one-dimensional defect around which some of the atoms are misaligned.
- **Pure edge dislocation:**
  - Visualized as an extra portion of a plane of atoms (an extra half-plane), the bottom edge of which terminates inside the crystal.
  - The line running along the bottom edge of the extra half-plane is the **dislocation line**.
  - Localized lattice distortion: atoms residing above the dislocation line are compressed (squeezed together), whereas atoms below the line are subjected to tensile strain (pulled apart).
  - Curvature of vertical planes diminishes with distance from the dislocation line; far from the line, the crystal lattice is virtually perfect.
  - Standard notation: designated by the symbol $\perp$ when the extra half-plane is in the top portion of the crystal, and $\top$ when the extra half-plane is in the bottom portion.
- **Pure screw dislocation:**
  - Formed by applying a shear stress that cuts and displaces the upper front region of the crystal by one atomic distance relative to the bottom portion.
  - The atomic planes trace a continuous spiral or helical ramp around the dislocation line.
  - Designated symbolically by a circular/semicircular arrow or screw symbol.
- **Mixed dislocations:**
  - Most dislocations in real crystalline solids are neither pure edge nor pure screw; rather, they exhibit components of both types and are termed **mixed dislocations**.
  - A curved dislocation line (Figure 4.6) transitions smoothly: at one exit surface (point $A$) it is pure screw, at another surface (point $B$) it is pure edge, and throughout the curved interior region it exhibits mixed edge and screw character.
- **The Burgers vector ($\mathbf{b}$):**
  - Specifies the exact magnitude and crystallographic direction of the lattice distortion associated with a dislocation.
  - **Geometric relationship between Burgers vector $\mathbf{b}$ and dislocation line:**
    - For an **edge dislocation**: $\mathbf{b}$ is **perpendicular** ($\perp$) to the dislocation line.
    - For a **screw dislocation**: $\mathbf{b}$ is **parallel** ($\parallel$) to the dislocation line.
    - For a **mixed dislocation**: $\mathbf{b}$ is **neither perpendicular nor parallel** to the dislocation line.
  - **Invariance of $\mathbf{b}$:** Even though a dislocation line curves and alters its character between edge, mixed, and screw, the Burgers vector $\mathbf{b}$ remains identical and invariant at every point along its entire length.
  - In metallic crystals, the Burgers vector points in a close-packed crystallographic direction, and its magnitude is equal to the interatomic spacing.
- **Role in plastic deformation:** Permanent (plastic) deformation in crystalline materials proceeds via the motion of dislocations (slip).
- **Observation:** Dislocations can be imaged directly using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), where they appear as dark, thread-like lines (Figure 4.7).
- **Origin of dislocations:** Introduced during solidification, during plastic deformation, and by thermal stresses induced during rapid cooling.

### Definitions
- **dislocation**: A linear or one-dimensional crystalline defect around which some of the atoms are misaligned.
- **edge dislocation**: A linear defect centered on the line along the terminal edge of an extra half-plane of atoms.
- **dislocation line**: The line that extends along the end of the extra half-plane of atoms for an edge dislocation, or along the center of the helical spiral for a screw dislocation.
- **screw dislocation**: A dislocation formed by a shear distortion producing a spiral or helical path or ramp of atomic planes around the dislocation line.
- **mixed dislocation**: A dislocation having both edge and screw character.
- **Burgers vector ($\mathbf{b}$)**: A vector that specifies the magnitude and direction of the lattice distortion associated with a dislocation.

## 4.6 Interfacial Defects

### Key ideas
- Interfacial defects are two-dimensional (planar) boundaries that typically separate regions having different crystal structures and/or crystallographic orientations.
- **Types of interfacial defects:**
  1. **External surfaces:**
     - The crystal structure terminates abruptly at an external surface.
     - Surface atoms lack the maximum number of nearest-neighbor bonds and thus reside in a higher energy state than interior atoms.
     - The unsatisfied dangling bonds give rise to a **surface energy** ($\text{J/m}^2$ or $\text{erg/cm}^2$).
     - Materials tend to minimize their total surface area to minimize surface free energy (e.g., liquid droplets assume a spherical shape). Solids cannot change shape spontaneously due to mechanical rigidity.
  2. **Grain boundaries:**
     - Interfacial regions several atom distances wide separating grains of differing crystallographic orientation in polycrystalline materials.
     - Atomic mismatch across the boundary leads to a **grain boundary energy** (similar to surface energy).
     - The magnitude of boundary energy increases with increasing degree of crystallographic misorientation:
       - **Small-angle (low-angle) grain boundaries:** Orientation mismatch is on the order of a few degrees. Can be described as regular arrays of dislocations:
         - *Tilt boundary:* Formed by an aligned vertical array of edge dislocations (Figure 4.9), producing misorientation angle $\theta$.
         - *Twist boundary:* Formed by an array of intersecting screw dislocations where the misorientation angle is parallel to the boundary plane.
       - **High-angle grain boundaries:** Orientation mismatch is large (Figure 4.8), resulting in severe atomic disorder.
     - Grain boundaries are chemically more reactive than grain interiors due to their excess boundary energy.
     - Solute/impurity atoms preferentially segregate along grain boundaries to reduce local strain energy.
     - Coarse-grained materials have less total boundary area per unit volume than fine-grained materials; thus, total interfacial energy is lower in coarse-grained materials.
     - Grains grow at elevated temperatures (grain growth) to reduce total boundary surface area and overall energy.
     - Despite atomic disorder, cohesive bonding across boundaries remains robust: polycrystalline metals exhibit high tensile strength, and their density is virtually identical to that of a single crystal of the same material.
  3. **Phase boundaries:**
     - Exist in multiphase materials where a different phase (with distinct physical/chemical properties and crystal structure) exists on each side of the boundary.
  4. **Twin boundaries:**
     - A special grain boundary across which there is a specific mirror lattice symmetry: atoms on one side reside in mirror-image positions relative to atoms on the other side (Figure 4.10).
     - The homogeneous lattice region between twin planes is called a twin.
     - **Mechanical twins:** Formed by atomic displacements induced by applied mechanical shear stresses; typical of BCC and HCP metals.
     - **Annealing twins:** Formed during recrystallization and annealing heat treatments following plastic deformation; typical of FCC metals (e.g., brass in Figure 4.14c).
     - Twinning occurs along specific crystallographic planes and directions characteristic of the crystal structure.
  5. **Miscellaneous interfacial defects:**
     - **Stacking faults:** Occur in FCC metals when the normal close-packed stacking sequence $ABCABCABC\dots$ is interrupted (e.g., $ABCABABC\dots$).
     - **Ferromagnetic domain walls:** Boundaries in ferromagnetic and ferrimagnetic materials separating regions with differing directions of spontaneous magnetization.
- **Interfacial energy hierarchy:**
  $$\text{External Surfaces} > \text{High-Angle Grain Boundaries} > \text{Small-Angle Grain Boundaries} > \text{Twin Boundaries} > \text{Domain Walls}$$

#### Materials of Importance 4.1: Catalysts (and Surface Defects)
- A **catalyst** accelerates the rate of a chemical reaction without being consumed.
- In solid heterogeneous catalysis, gas or liquid reactant molecules **adsorb** onto active sites on the solid catalyst surface, enhancing their chemical reactivity.
- **Adsorption vs. absorption:** Adsorption is the surface adhesion of molecules; absorption is the assimilation of molecules into the interior of a bulk solid or liquid.
- Adsorption sites are primarily **surface defects** on atomic planes: ledges, kinks, terraces, vacancies, and individual adatoms (Figure 4.11).
- **Automotive catalytic converters:**
  - Located in automobile exhaust systems to reduce toxic emissions: carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$), nitrogen oxides ($\text{NO}_x$), and unburned hydrocarbons.
  - Exhaust gases mix with air and pass over a washcoated catalyst substrate, commonly $(\text{Ce}_{0.5}\text{Zr}_{0.5})\text{O}_2$.
  - Catalytic reactions on surface defects:
    - $\text{NO}_x$ adsorbs and dissociates into atomic $\text{N}$ and $\text{O}$; pairs of nitrogen atoms combine to produce non-toxic $\text{N}_2$ gas.
    - $\text{O}_2$ dissociates into oxygen atoms, which oxidize $\text{CO}$ to form $\text{CO}_2$.
    - Unburned hydrocarbons are oxidized completely to $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
  - Figure 4.12 shows a high-resolution TEM image of $(\text{Ce}_{0.5}\text{Zr}_{0.5})\text{O}_2$ single crystals resolving individual atom columns and catalytic surface defect sites.

### Definitions
- **interfacial defects**: Two-dimensional boundaries that separate regions of materials having different crystal structures and/or crystallographic orientations.
- **external surface**: The interfacial boundary along which a crystal structure terminates, characterized by unsatisfied atomic bonds and excess surface energy.
- **surface energy**: The excess free energy per unit area ($\text{J/m}^2$) associated with the unsaturated bonds of surface atoms.
- **grain boundary**: A two-dimensional interfacial defect separating two grains of differing crystallographic orientation in a polycrystalline material.
- **small-angle (low-angle) grain boundary**: A grain boundary across which the crystallographic orientation mismatch between adjacent grains is only a few degrees.
- **tilt boundary**: A small-angle grain boundary formed by a vertical array of aligned edge dislocations.
- **twist boundary**: A small-angle grain boundary formed by an array of screw dislocations where the misorientation axis is parallel to the boundary plane.
- **high-angle grain boundary**: A grain boundary across which the crystallographic misorientation is large.
- **phase boundary**: An interfacial boundary separating two distinct physical or chemical phases in a multiphase material.
- **twin boundary**: A special grain boundary across which there is a specific mirror lattice symmetry such that atoms on one side are in mirror-image positions to atoms on the other side.
- **mechanical twins**: Twins produced by atomic displacements resulting from applied mechanical shear forces (predominant in BCC and HCP).
- **annealing twins**: Twins formed during annealing heat treatments following plastic deformation (predominant in FCC).
- **stacking fault**: An interfacial defect in FCC crystals resulting from an error or interruption in the close-packed $ABCABC\dots$ stacking sequence.
- **domain wall**: The interfacial boundary separating adjacent magnetic domains that have different directions of magnetization.
- **catalyst**: A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without participating in or being consumed by the reaction itself.
- **adsorption**: The adhesion of molecules of a gas or liquid to the surface of a solid.
- **adatom**: An individual atom adsorbed upon a flat crystal terrace surface.

## 4.7 Bulk or Volume Defects

### Key ideas
- Solid materials contain three-dimensional bulk or volume defects that are much larger in scale than point, linear, or interfacial defects.
- Examples of bulk defects include:
  - **Pores:** Internal gaseous voids or cavities.
  - **Cracks:** Microscopic or macroscopic planar separations that severely compromise tensile strength.
  - **Foreign inclusions:** Entrapped non-metallic particles (such as oxides, sulfides, or silicates).
  - **Other phases:** Inadvertent or secondary second-phase precipitates.
- Bulk defects are typically introduced during processing and fabrication steps (e.g., casting solidification shrinkage, gas entrapment during welding, compaction voids during powder metallurgy, or microcracking during heat treatment).
- Bulk defects act as severe stress concentrators and can drastically degrade the mechanical integrity of structural components.

### Definitions
- **bulk or volume defects**: Three-dimensional macroscopic or microscopic imperfections in solids, including pores, cracks, foreign inclusions, and second-phase particles.

## 4.8 Atomic Vibrations

### Key ideas
- Every atom in a solid material undergoes rapid, continuous vibrations around its equilibrium lattice position.
- Atomic vibrations may be viewed as dynamic crystalline imperfections because they disrupt perfect spatial periodicity.
- At any given instant, not all atoms vibrate at the same frequency, amplitude, or energy.
- At a given temperature, a Maxwell-Boltzmann-like distribution of vibrational energies exists among the constituent atoms around an average energy.
- Over time, the vibrational energy of any specific individual atom fluctuates randomly as energy is exchanged between neighbouring bonds.
- The average vibrational energy increases monotonically with rising temperature. In physical terms, the absolute temperature of a solid is simply a direct macroscopic measure of the average vibrational activity of its atoms and molecules.
- Typical room-temperature vibrational parameters:
  - Frequency: on the order of $10^{13}\text{ vibrations per second}$ ($10^{13}\text{ s}^{-1}$).
  - Amplitude: a few thousandths of a nanometer ($\sim 10^{-3}\text{ nm} = 1\text{ pm}$).
- Many physical properties and transformations in solids are direct consequences of atomic vibrations. For example, solid-state melting occurs when thermal vibrations become sufficiently violent to rupture large numbers of interatomic bonds simultaneously.

### Definitions
- **atomic vibration**: The rapid, temperature-dependent oscillation of atoms around their equilibrium lattice sites within a solid.

## 4.9 Basic Concepts of Microscopy

### Key ideas
- Microstructural examination is used to observe the structural features and defects that govern material properties.
- **Macroscopic vs. microscopic features:**
  - Some structural features are macroscopic (visible to the unaided eye). For example, large grains are visible on galvanized aluminum streetlight poles, highway guardrails, and sectioned copper ingots (Figure 4.13).
  - In most engineering materials, grains are microscopic, with diameters on the order of microns ($1\,\mu\text{m} = 10^{-6}\text{ m}$), requiring specialized microscopes.
- **Microstructure** encompasses grain size, grain shape, phase distribution, defect arrangements, and other microscopic structural characteristics.
- **Microscopy** employs optical, electron, and scanning probe microscopes. Images recorded via photographic or digital equipment are termed **photomicrographs**.
- **Key engineering applications of microstructural characterization:**
  1. Confirming structure–property–processing relationships.
  2. Predicting material performance and service lifetime.
  3. Designing new alloys with tailored property combinations.
  4. Verifying correct thermal processing and heat-treatment specifications.
  5. Conducting failure analysis to determine mechanical fracture modes (e.g., ductile vs. brittle, fatigue, intergranular failure).

### Definitions
- **microstructure**: Structural elements and defects that are of microscopic dimensions, including grain size, shape, and phase distribution.
- **microscopy**: The observation and investigation of microstructure using an optical, electron, or scanning probe microscope.
- **photomicrograph**: A photographic or digitally captured record of an image produced by a microscope.

## 4.10 Microscopic Techniques

### Key ideas
- **Optical Microscopy (Light Microscopy):**
  - Uses visible light and optical glass lenses in reflecting mode (metallography) for opaque materials (metals, ceramics, polymers). Image contrast arises from differences in surface reflectivity.
  - *Specimen preparation:* Requires grinding and mechanical polishing using successively finer abrasive papers (silicon carbide) and diamond/alumina powders to produce a mirror-like finish.
  - *Chemical etching:* Application of a chemical reagent (e.g., dilute acid) that differentially attacks the surface based on crystallographic orientation:
    - *Grain contrast:* Etching produces tiny facets whose angle depends on grain orientation. Grains reflecting light directly into the objective lens appear bright; grains reflecting light away appear dark or gray (Figure 4.14).
    - *Grain boundary contrast:* Boundary atoms have higher energy and dissolve faster, creating shallow V-shaped microscopic grooves along grain boundaries. These grooves scatter light away from the objective, making grain boundaries appear as dark lines under the microscope (Figure 4.15).
    - *Two-phase contrast:* Selected etchants preferentially stain or dissolve one phase, producing texture or contrast differences that distinguish phases.
  - Upper magnification limit: approximately $2000\times$, limited by the wavelength of visible light ($\sim 500\text{ nm}$).
- **Electron Microscopy:**
  - Employs accelerated high-velocity electron beams instead of light. Electrons exhibit wavelike behavior (de Broglie hypothesis) with wavelength inversely proportional to velocity:
    $$\lambda \propto \frac{1}{v}$$
  - Accelerated across high voltages, electron wavelengths reach $\approx 0.003\text{ nm}$ ($3\text{ pm}$), yielding orders-of-magnitude higher resolving power and magnification than optical microscopes.
  - Magnetic coils act as electromagnetic lenses to focus the beam.
  - **Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM):**
    - An electron beam passes through an ultra-thin foil specimen.
    - Image contrast arises from localized differences in electron scattering or diffraction produced by defects and microstructural features.
    - Specimen preparation is challenging: foils must be thinned to tens of nanometers to permit electron transmission.
    - Transmitted electrons strike a fluorescent viewing screen or digital detector.
    - Magnifications approach $1,000,000\times$; primary tool for observing individual dislocations (Figure 4.7).
  - **Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM):**
    - A finely focused electron beam raster-scans the specimen surface.
    - Reflected (backscattered) and emitted secondary electrons are collected by a detector and displayed synchronously on a cathode ray tube (CRT) or digital monitor.
    - The specimen surface must be electrically conductive to prevent charge accumulation; non-conductive specimens receive an ultra-thin sputtered conductive metallic coating (e.g., gold-palladium).
    - Magnifications range from $10\times$ to $> 50,000\times$.
    - Characterized by very large depth of field, giving images a distinct three-dimensional appearance.
    - Accessory equipment (energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, EDS/EDX) enables localized chemical composition analysis.
- **Scanning Probe Microscopy (SPM):**
  - A modern class of microscopes that uses neither light nor electron beams.
  - Generates atomic-scale three-dimensional topographical maps of specimen surfaces.
  - Key operational features:
    - Magnifications up to $10^9\times$, providing sub-nanometer spatial resolution capable of resolving single atoms and vacancies.
    - Direct generation of true 3D surface topography.
    - Operates in diverse environments: vacuum, ambient air, or immersed in liquids.
  - *Mechanism:* A sharp probe tip is positioned within $\sim 1\text{ nm}$ of the surface and raster-scanned across the specimen. The probe experiences minute vertical deflections driven by atomic forces or tunneling currents. Piezoelectric ceramic actuators control lateral and vertical movements with picometer precision, while computer software reconstructs the topographical image.
  - Critical tool in the development of nanotechnology and nanomaterials.
- **Scale and Resolution Comparison (Figure 4.16):**
  - Structural feature dimensions span over $12$ orders of magnitude: subatomic particles ($< 10^{-14}\text{ m}$), atom/ion diameters ($\sim 10^{-10}\text{ m} = 0.1\text{ nm}$), unit cell edges ($10^{-10}$–$10^{-9}\text{ m}$), dislocation widths ($10^{-9}$–$10^{-8}\text{ m}$), second phase particles ($10^{-8}$–$10^{-5}\text{ m}$), grains ($10^{-6}$–$10^{-3}\text{ m}$), and macrostructural cracks/pores ($10^{-4}$–$10^{-1}\text{ m}$).
  - Useful resolution ranges: SPM ($10^{-10}$–$10^{-6}\text{ m}$), TEM ($10^{-10}$–$10^{-6}\text{ m}$), SEM ($10^{-9}$–$10^{-3}\text{ m}$), Optical microscope ($10^{-7}$–$10^{-3}\text{ m}$), Naked eye ($10^{-4}$–$1\text{ m}$).

### Definitions
- **optical microscopy**: Microstructural characterization using visible light in transmission or reflection mode with glass optical lenses.
- **metallographic**: Pertaining to the preparation and microscopic examination of polished and etched metal surfaces.
- **etching**: Chemical treatment of a polished metallurgical specimen using an acid or chemical reagent to reveal grains, grain boundaries, and phases through differential dissolution and reflectivity.
- **transmission electron microscope (TEM)**: A microscope that produces magnified images by transmitting an accelerated electron beam through an ultra-thin specimen, achieving magnifications up to $1,000,000\times$.
- **scanning electron microscope (SEM)**: A microscope that rasters a focused electron beam across an electrically conductive specimen surface to produce high-depth-of-field images from backscattered and secondary electrons at magnifications from $10\times$ to $> 50,000\times$.
- **scanning probe microscope (SPM)**: A microscope that rasters an atomically sharp physical tip within nanometer proximity of a specimen surface to generate three-dimensional topographical maps with magnifications up to $10^9\times$.

## 4.11 Grain-Size Determination

### Key ideas
- Grain size is an important structural parameter in polycrystalline materials because mechanical strength and ductility depend strongly on grain diameter (Hall-Petch relationship).
- Constituent grains have irregular geometric shapes and a distribution of sizes; therefore, grain size is expressed as a mean or average grain diameter.
- Modern grain-size determination is automated using digital image analysis software to measure boundary lengths, intercept counts, and grain areas.
- **Manual methods using photomicrographs:**
  1. **Linear Intercept Method:**
     - Straight test lines are drawn randomly across several photomicrographs taken at the same magnification $M$.
     - The total number of grain boundary intersections ($P$) across all lines is counted.
     - The mean intercept length $\ell$ (in real space, $1\times$) is calculated:
       $$\ell = \frac{L_T}{P M}$$
  2. **ASTM Comparison Method (ASTM Standard E112):**
     - Uses standardized charts with assigned **grain-size numbers ($G$)** ranging from $1$ (coarse) to $10$ (very fine), referenced to photomicrographs taken at a magnification of $100\times$.
     - The grain-size number $G$ is defined by:
       $$n = 2^{G - 1}$$
       where $n$ is the average number of grains per square inch at $100\times$.
     - For micrographs taken at magnifications other than $100\times$, a scaling factor $(M/100)^2$ is applied:
       $$n_M \left(\frac{M}{100}\right)^2 = 2^{G - 1} \implies n_M = 2^{G - 1}\left(\frac{100}{M}\right)^2$$
       where $n_M$ is the number of grains per square inch at magnification $M$. The quadratic factor accounts for area scaling with linear magnification.
     - **Converting mean intercept length $\ell$ directly to ASTM grain-size number $G$:**
       - For $\ell$ in millimeters: $G = -6.6457 \log \ell - 3.298$
       - For $\ell$ in inches: $G = -6.6353 \log \ell - 12.6$
- **Determining magnification from a scale bar:**
  - A scale bar is a line of known physical length (usually in microns) superimposed on a micrograph.
  - To calculate magnification $M$:
    1. Measure the scale bar length in millimeters with a ruler.
    2. Convert measured millimeters to microns ($\times 1000$).
    3. Divide by the physical dimension indicated by the scale bar:
       $$M = \frac{\text{measured scale length in }\mu\text{m}}{\text{indicated scale bar value in }\mu\text{m}}$$

### Definitions
- **grain size**: The average or mean diameter of the constituent grains in a polycrystalline material.
- **linear intercept method**: A technique for determining grain size by counting the number of grain boundary intersections per unit length of test lines drawn across a photomicrograph.
- **mean intercept length ($\ell$)**: A quantitative measure of average grain diameter in real space, defined as $\ell = L_T / (PM)$.
- **comparison method**: A grain-size measurement procedure comparing micrographs at $100\times$ against standardized ASTM charts.
- **ASTM grain-size number ($G$)**: A standardized index ranging from $1$ to $10$ representing grain size, related to grain density by $n = 2^{G-1}$ at $100\times$.
- **scale bar**: A line of calibrated physical dimension placed on a micrograph to determine linear magnification.

### Equations
- **Equation (4.16):** Mean intercept length
  $$\ell = \frac{L_T}{P M}$$
  - $\ell$: mean intercept length in real space (mm or $\mu\text{m}$)
  - $L_T$: total length of all test lines drawn on the photomicrograph (mm)
  - $P$: total number of grain boundary intersections counted across all lines
  - $M$: linear magnification of the photomicrograph (dimensionless)
- **Equation (4.17):** ASTM grain-size relationship at $100\times$ magnification
  $$n = 2^{G - 1}$$
  - $n$: average number of grains per square inch at $100\times$ magnification ($\text{in}^{-2}$)
  - $G$: ASTM grain-size number (dimensionless integer or decimal)
- **Equation (4.18):** ASTM grain-size relationship at non-standard magnification $M$
  $$n_M \left(\frac{M}{100}\right)^2 = 2^{G - 1}$$
  $$\text{or equivalently: } n_M = (2^{G - 1})\left(\frac{100}{M}\right)^2$$
  - $n_M$: average number of grains per square inch at magnification $M$ ($\text{in}^{-2}$)
  - $M$: actual magnification of the micrograph
  - $G$: ASTM grain-size number
- **Equation (4.19a):** Relationship between ASTM grain-size number and mean intercept length in millimeters
  $$G = -6.6457 \log \ell - 3.298 \quad (\text{for } \ell \text{ in mm})$$
  - $G$: ASTM grain-size number
  - $\ell$: mean intercept length in millimeters
  - $\log$: common logarithm (base 10)
- **Equation (4.19b):** Relationship between ASTM grain-size number and mean intercept length in inches
  $$G = -6.6353 \log \ell - 12.6 \quad (\text{for } \ell \text{ in inches})$$
  - $G$: ASTM grain-size number
  - $\ell$: mean intercept length in inches
- **Equation (4.20):** Calculation of magnification from a scale bar
  $$M = \frac{\text{measured scale length (converted to microns)}}{\text{the number appearing by the scale bar (in microns)}}$$
  - $M$: linear magnification

### Worked examples
- **Example Problem 4.5: Grain-Size Computations Using ASTM and Intercept Methods**
  - *Problem Statement:* "The following is a schematic micrograph that represents the microstructure of some hypothetical metal. Determine the following: (a) Mean intercept length (b) ASTM grain-size number, G using Equation 4.19a"
  - *Solution:*
    - **(a) Mean Intercept Length:**
      1. Determine magnification $M$ from the scale bar using Equation 4.20:
         $$\text{Measured scale bar length} = 16\text{ mm} = 16,000\,\mu\text{m}$$
         $$\text{Scale bar indicated length} = 100\,\mu\text{m}$$
         $$M = \frac{16,000\,\mu\text{m}}{100\,\mu\text{m}} = 160\times$$
      2. Calculate total test line length $L_T$:
         $$L_T = (7\text{ lines})(50\text{ mm/line}) = 350\text{ mm}$$
      3. Sum the grain boundary intersections from the data table:
         $$P = 8 + 8 + 8 + 9 + 9 + 9 + 7 = 58\text{ intersections}$$
      4. Compute mean intercept length $\ell$ using Equation 4.16:
         $$\ell = \frac{L_T}{P M} = \frac{350\text{ mm}}{(58)(160)} = \frac{350}{9280} = 0.0377\text{ mm} \quad (37.7\,\mu\text{m})$$
    - **(b) ASTM Grain-Size Number ($G$):**
      - Substitute $\ell = 0.0377\text{ mm}$ into Equation 4.19a:
        $$G = -6.6457 \log \ell - 3.298$$
        $$G = -6.6457 \log(0.0377) - 3.298$$
        $$\log(0.0377) = -1.42366$$
        $$G = (-6.6457)(-1.42366) - 3.298 = 9.4611 - 3.298 = 6.16$$

### Concept checks
- **Concept Check 4.4**
  - *Question verbatim:* "Does the grain-size number (G of Equation 4.17) increase or decrease with decreasing grain size? Why?"
  - *Answer:* The grain-size number $G$ increases with decreasing grain size. When grain size decreases, individual grains become smaller, meaning a greater number of grains are packed into a unit area (i.e., the number of grains per square inch $n$ increases). According to Equation 4.17 ($n = 2^{G-1}$), $n$ is an increasing function of $G$; therefore, an increase in grain density $n$ directly requires an increase in the ASTM grain-size number $G$.

## Summary & key terms

### Chapter 3 Summary
*(Reproduced from textbook pp. 88–89 of excerpt)*

- **Fundamental Concepts:** Atoms in crystalline solids are positioned in orderly and repeated patterns that are in contrast to the random and disordered atomic distribution found in noncrystalline or amorphous materials.
- **Unit Cells:** Crystal structures are specified in terms of parallelepiped unit cells, which are characterized by geometry and atom positions within.
- **Metallic Crystal Structures:**
  - Most common metals exist in at least one of three relatively simple crystal structures:
    - Face-centered cubic (FCC), which has a cubic unit cell.
    - Body-centered cubic (BCC), which also has a cubic unit cell.
    - Hexagonal close-packed (HCP), which has a unit cell of hexagonal symmetry.
  - Two features of a crystal structure are:
    - Coordination number: the number of nearest-neighbor atoms.
    - Atomic packing factor (APF): the fraction of solid sphere volume in the unit cell.
- **Point Coordinates, Crystallographic Directions, and Crystallographic Planes:**
  - Crystallographic points, directions, and planes are specified in terms of indexing schemes based on coordinate axes defined by the unit cell.
  - Point coordinates are fractional multiples of cell edge lengths (Equations 3.9a–3.9c).
  - Directional indices are computed as differences between vector head and tail coordinates (Equations 3.10a–3.10c).
  - Planar (Miller) indices are determined from the reciprocals of axial intercepts (Equations 3.13a–3.13c).
  - For hexagonal unit cells, a four-index scheme ($[uvtw]$ and $(hkil)$) is used (Equations 3.11a–3.11d, 3.12a–3.12c).
- **Linear and Planar Densities:**
  - Directional and planar equivalencies correlate with atomic linear and planar densities.
  - Planes with identical atomic packing belonging to the same crystal structure constitute a family of planes.
- **Close-Packed Crystal Structures:**
  - FCC and HCP structures are generated by stacking close-packed planes of atoms ($ABABAB\dots$ for HCP, $ABCABCABC\dots$ for FCC).
- **Single Crystals & Polycrystalline Materials:**
  - Single crystals exhibit uninterrupted periodic atomic order across the entire specimen, sometimes displaying flat faces and regular geometric shapes.
  - The vast majority of crystalline solids are polycrystalline, composed of many small crystals or grains with different orientations.
  - A grain boundary is the boundary region separating two grains where atomic mismatch occurs.
- **Anisotropy:** Anisotropy is the directional dependence of properties. In isotropic materials, properties are independent of measurement direction.
- **X-Ray Diffraction (Determination of Crystal Structures):**
  - X-ray diffractometry determines crystal structures and interplanar spacings through constructive interference between X-rays and parallel atomic planes.
  - Bragg's law ($n\lambda = 2d_{hkl}\sin\theta$, Equation 3.20) specifies the condition for diffraction.
- **Noncrystalline Solids:** Noncrystalline solids lack a systematic, regular arrangement of atoms over relatively large atomic distances. They are also referred to as amorphous materials.

### Chapter 4 Summary
*(Reproduced from textbook pp. 118–119 of excerpt)*

- **Vacancies and Self-Interstitials:**
  - Point defects are associated with one or two atomic positions, including vacancies (vacant lattice sites) and self-interstitials (host atoms occupying interstitial sites).
  - The equilibrium number of vacancies depends exponentially on temperature according to Equation 4.1 ($N_v = N \exp[-Q_v/kT]$).
- **Impurities in Solids:**
  - An alloy is a metallic substance composed of two or more elements.
  - A solid solution forms when impurity atoms are added to a host solid such that the original crystal structure is retained and no new phases form.
  - In substitutional solid solutions, solute atoms replace host atoms.
  - In interstitial solid solutions, relatively small impurity atoms occupy interstitial void spaces among host atoms.
  - High substitutional solid solubility occurs when the four Hume-Rothery rules are satisfied.
- **Specification of Composition:**
  - Alloy composition can be expressed in weight percent (mass fraction, Equations 4.3a, 4.3b) or atom percent (mole/atom fraction, Equations 4.5a, 4.5b).
  - Mathematical conversions allow interconversion between weight percent and atom percent (Equations 4.6a, 4.7a).
- **Dislocations—Linear Defects:**
  - Dislocations are one-dimensional crystalline defects consisting of two pure forms: edge and screw.
    - Edge dislocation: lattice distortion along the terminus of an extra half-plane of atoms.
    - Screw dislocation: helical planar ramp of atoms.
    - Mixed dislocation: possesses both edge and screw components.
  - The magnitude and direction of lattice distortion are specified by the Burgers vector $\mathbf{b}$.
  - The Burgers vector is perpendicular to the dislocation line for edge, parallel for screw, and at an oblique angle for mixed dislocations.
- **Interfacial Defects:**
  - Within a grain boundary (several atomic distances wide), atomic mismatch exists between adjacent grains of differing orientation.
  - Across a twin boundary, atoms on one side reside in mirror-image positions of atoms on the opposite side.
- **Microscopic Techniques:**
  - Microstructure consists of structural features and defects of microscopic dimensions. Microscopy is the observation of microstructure.
  - Optical and electron microscopes operate in both transmissive and reflective modes.
  - Electron microscopes include:
    - Transmission electron microscope (TEM): image formed by an electron beam passing through an ultra-thin foil, utilizing scattering/diffraction contrast.
    - Scanning electron microscope (SEM): raster-scans the specimen surface with an electron beam, producing images from reflected/backscattered electrons.
  - Scanning probe microscope (SPM): rasters a sharp physical probe across the surface, generating 3D topographical images with nanometer-scale resolution.
- **Grain-Size Determination:**
  - Linear intercept method: straight test lines drawn across a photomicrograph; intersections counted; mean intercept length $\ell$ computed via Equation 4.16 ($\ell = L_T / PM$).
  - Comparison method: comparison of $100\times$ micrographs against ASTM standard charts to determine grain-size number $G$ via Equation 4.17 ($n = 2^{G-1}$).
