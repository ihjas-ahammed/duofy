Module I · 15 hours · 20 marks · Puri & Babbar §1.1–1.13, 2.1–2.2, 2.4, 2.7

Crystal Structure

Five units, almost entirely new. The whole module is one definition unfolded: a crystal is a lattice plus a basis. Symmetry, Miller indices, packing fractions and diffraction are all consequences of that repetition.

Unit 1

Crystal lattice, translation vectors, unit cell, basis

You carry over

That solids are made of atoms, and that some solids look like regular shapes. Nothing formal.

New ground
  • The lattice as a mathematical set of points, separate from the atoms
  • Translational invariance as the defining property
  • Primitive vs conventional cells, and the Wigner–Seitz cell
  • Crystal = lattice + basis

Crystalline solids have long-range periodic order; amorphous solids (glass, rubber) have only short-range order and no sharp melting point. A crystal may be a single crystal or polycrystalline (many grains).

The space lattice $$\mathbf R_n=n_1\mathbf a+n_2\mathbf b+n_3\mathbf c,\qquad n_1,n_2,n_3\in\mathbb Z$$

An infinite array of points in which every point has identical surroundings. \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c\) are the primitive translation vectors. The crystal looks exactly the same viewed from \(\mathbf r\) and from \(\mathbf r+\mathbf R_n\) — that is translational invariance, and it is the only assumption the rest of the module needs.

Basis and structure $$\textbf{crystal structure}=\textbf{space lattice}+\textbf{basis}$$

The basis is the group of atoms attached to every lattice point — identical in composition, arrangement and orientation. One atom per point for Cu; two for diamond and NaCl; thousands for a protein crystal.

  • Primitive cell: smallest volume that fills space by translation; contains exactly one lattice point. Not unique in shape.
  • Conventional (non-primitive) cell: larger, chosen to display the symmetry — the cube used for BCC and FCC contains 2 and 4 points.
  • Wigner–Seitz cell: bisect the lines to all neighbouring lattice points with planes; the smallest enclosed volume is a primitive cell that also carries the full point symmetry. Its counterpart in the reciprocal lattice is the first Brillouin zone — remember this now and Unit 5 costs you nothing.
Unit 2

Symmetry operations, point groups and space groups

You carry over

Line and rotational symmetry of plane figures from school geometry.

New ground
  • The five symmetry elements a crystal may possess
  • The proof that only 1-, 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold axes are possible
  • 32 point groups and 230 space groups

A symmetry operation leaves the crystal indistinguishable from its original state. In a periodic lattice the allowed operations are: translation, rotation about an axis, reflection in a plane, inversion through a centre, and rotoinversion (rotation followed by inversion).

DeriveWhy five-fold symmetry is impossible5 marks · a favourite
Step 1 · set up a rowTake a row of lattice points spaced \(t\). Rotate the whole lattice by \(+\phi\) about one point \(A\) and by \(-\phi\) about its neighbour \(B\). Since these are symmetry operations, the images \(A'\) and \(B'\) must also be lattice points.
Step 2 · measure the new row\(A'\) and \(B'\) lie on a line parallel to \(AB\), and by the geometry of the construction $$A'B'=t+2t\cos\phi$$
Step 3 · impose periodicity\(A'B'\) joins two lattice points on a row of spacing \(t\), so it must be an integer multiple: $$t+2t\cos\phi=mt\;\Longrightarrow\;2\cos\phi=m-1\equiv M,\quad M\in\mathbb Z$$
Step 4 · enumerate\(|\cos\phi|\le1\) forces \(M=-2,-1,0,1,2\), hence $$\phi=180^\circ,\;120^\circ,\;90^\circ,\;60^\circ,\;360^\circ$$ i.e. only \(n=2,3,4,6,1\). Five-fold and seven-fold axes cannot tile the plane — which is exactly why regular pentagons leave gaps. (Quasicrystals show five-fold diffraction symmetry precisely because they are not periodic.)

Point group: the set of symmetry operations that leave at least one point fixed (rotations, reflections, inversion, rotoinversion). There are 32 crystallographic point groups. Space group: point-group operations combined with translations, including screw axes and glide planes — 230 of them in three dimensions. In two dimensions: 10 point groups and 17 space groups.

Counting points per conventional cell $$N=N_i+\frac{N_f}{2}+\frac{N_c}{8}$$

Interior points belong to one cell, face points are shared by two, corner points by eight. So SC \(=1\), BCC \(=1+8/8=2\), FCC \(=6/2+8/8=4\).

The four brackets

\([uvw]\) one direction · \(\langle uvw\rangle\) all symmetry-equivalent directions · \((hkl)\) one set of parallel planes · \(\{hkl\}\) all equivalent sets. Negative indices carry an overbar. In a cubic crystal — and only there — \([hkl]\) is normal to \((hkl)\).

Miller recipe: ① find the intercepts in units of \(a,b,c\) ② take reciprocals ③ clear fractions to the smallest integers. A plane parallel to an axis has intercept \(\infty\), hence index 0.

DeriveInterplanar spacing4 marks
Step 1 · the nearest plane to the originThe \((hkl)\) plane closest to the origin cuts the axes at \(a/h,\;b/k,\;c/l\). Let \(d\) be the perpendicular distance from the origin to it, with direction cosines \(\cos\alpha,\cos\beta,\cos\gamma\).
Step 2 · direction cosines from the intercepts$$\cos\alpha=\frac{d}{a/h}=\frac{dh}{a},\qquad \cos\beta=\frac{dk}{b},\qquad \cos\gamma=\frac{dl}{c}$$
Step 3 · use the identityFor orthogonal axes \(\cos^2\alpha+\cos^2\beta+\cos^2\gamma=1\), so $$d^2\left[\frac{h^2}{a^2}+\frac{k^2}{b^2}+\frac{l^2}{c^2}\right]=1$$

Compound structures — all built on FCC

Diamond: FCC lattice, basis of two identical atoms at \((0,0,0)\) and \((\frac14,\frac14,\frac14)\); nearest-neighbour distance \(\frac{\sqrt3}{4}a\).
Zinc blende (ZnS): the same geometry, but the two basis atoms are different elements; \(CN=4{:}4\), 4 formula units per cell. GaAs, InSb.

CsCl: simple cubic lattice with a two-ion basis — not BCC, because the two ions are different; \(CN=8{:}8\), 1 formula unit per cell.

Trap

CsCl is often called "BCC" — it is not a BCC lattice, because the centre ion differs from the corner ions and so the two sites do not have identical surroundings. The lattice is simple cubic with a two-ion basis. The same distinction makes diamond an FCC lattice with a two-atom basis, not a lattice of its own.

Checkpoint · say it out loudCopper is FCC with \(a=3.61\) Å. Find the atomic radius and the packing fraction.

Atoms touch along the face diagonal, whose length is \(\sqrt2 a\) and which contains \(4r\):
\(r=\dfrac{\sqrt2 a}{4}=\dfrac{1.414\times3.61}{4}=\) 1.28 Å.

\(f=\dfrac{4\times\frac43\pi r^3}{a^3}=\dfrac{\pi}{3\sqrt2}=\) 0.74 — independent of \(a\), as it must be, since it is a pure geometry factor.
Unit 5

X-ray diffraction, the reciprocal lattice and Brillouin zones

You carry over

Interference of waves and the condition for constructive interference — path difference \(=n\lambda\).

New ground
  • Bragg's law, and Laue's equivalent formulation
  • The reciprocal lattice, and why \(|\mathbf G_{hkl}|=2\pi/d_{hkl}\)
  • Systematic absences — how the pattern tells you SC from BCC from FCC
  • Brillouin zone = Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice

X-rays are used because \(\lambda\sim1\) Å matches interatomic spacing: \(2d\sin\theta=n\lambda\) has no solution unless \(\lambda\le2d\). Visible light simply cannot see a lattice.

Bragg's law $$2d\sin\theta=n\lambda$$

Treat the planes as mirrors. Two rays reflecting from adjacent planes differ in path by \(2d\sin\theta\); constructive interference needs this to be a whole number of wavelengths. \(\theta\) is measured from the plane, not from the normal — the standard slip.

Reciprocal lattice $$\mathbf b_1=2\pi\frac{\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3}{\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)},\quad\text{and cyclically};\qquad \mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf a_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$$ $$\mathbf G_{hkl}=h\mathbf b_1+k\mathbf b_2+l\mathbf b_3,\qquad \mathbf G_{hkl}\perp(hkl),\qquad |\mathbf G_{hkl}|=\frac{2\pi}{d_{hkl}}$$

Each point of the reciprocal lattice stands for a whole set of planes of the real crystal. Reciprocal of SC is SC (side \(2\pi/a\)); reciprocal of BCC is FCC and of FCC is BCC — a two-mark question on its own.

DeriveLaue condition, and why it is Bragg's law5 marks
Step 1 · elastic scatteringIncident wave vector \(\mathbf k\), scattered \(\mathbf k'\), with \(|\mathbf k|=|\mathbf k'|=2\pi/\lambda\) (no energy lost). Define the scattering vector \(\Delta\mathbf k=\mathbf k'-\mathbf k\).
Step 2 · the Laue conditionSumming the scattered amplitude over all lattice points gives a non-zero result only when the phase factor \(e^{i\Delta\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}=1\) for every lattice vector \(\mathbf R\) — that is, when $$\Delta\mathbf k=\mathbf G$$ for some reciprocal lattice vector \(\mathbf G\). Diffraction happens only in directions that reach a reciprocal lattice point.
Step 3 · square it\(\mathbf k'=\mathbf k+\mathbf G\) with \(|\mathbf k'|=|\mathbf k|\) gives \(k^2=k^2+2\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G+G^2\), so $$2\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G+G^2=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2\mathbf k\cdot(-\mathbf G)=G^2$$
Step 4 · recover BraggWrite \(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G=kG\sin\theta\) with \(\theta\) the angle between \(\mathbf k\) and the plane, and put \(G=2\pi n/d\), \(k=2\pi/\lambda\): $$2\cdot\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\sin\theta=\frac{2\pi n}{d}\;\Longrightarrow\;\boxed{2d\sin\theta=n\lambda}$$ Same physics, two languages: Bragg is convenient for numbers, Laue for theory.
Step 5 · the geometric payoff\(2\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G=G^2\) says \(\mathbf k\) ends on the perpendicular bisector plane of \(\mathbf G\). Those bisector planes are exactly the Brillouin zone boundaries — so a wave is Bragg-reflected precisely when its wave vector reaches a zone boundary. That single sentence links Module I to the band gaps of Module II.
Structure factor and systematic absences $$F_{hkl}=\sum_j f_j\,e^{2\pi i(hx_j+ky_j+lz_j)},\qquad I\propto|F_{hkl}|^2$$

SC: all \((hkl)\) present. BCC: \(F=f\left[1+e^{i\pi(h+k+l)}\right]\) — present only when \(h+k+l\) is even. FCC: present only when \(h,k,l\) are all even or all odd (unmixed). So the first few lines are 100,110,111… for SC; 110,200,211… for BCC; 111,200,220,311… for FCC. Reading off which lines are missing is how a powder pattern identifies the lattice.

Brillouin zones. The first BZ is the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice — the region reachable from the origin without crossing a bisector plane. For a 2D square lattice of side \(a\) it is a square of side \(2\pi/a\); for a real-space FCC crystal (reciprocal BCC) it is a truncated octahedron, and for BCC (reciprocal FCC) a rhombic dodecahedron.

Theory of Solids

Three theories in succession, each fixing what the last one broke: Drude's classical gas, Sommerfeld's quantum gas, and finally bands — which are the only way to explain why anything is an insulator.

Unit 6

Drude–Lorentz classical free electron theory

You carry over

Ohm's law, drift velocity, and the kinetic theory of an ideal gas (\(\frac32k_BT\) per particle, Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics).

New ground
  • Valence electrons treated as a gas inside the metal
  • \(\sigma=ne^2\tau/m\) derived, and Wiedemann–Franz
  • Four specific failures — the examinable list
  • Assumptions: valence electrons are free inside the metal; the ion cores and mutual repulsion are ignored; electrons obey Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics with \(\frac32k_BT\) each; they are scattered by the ions with a mean free time \(\tau\).
Successes $$\mathbf v_d=-\frac{e\tau}{m}\mathbf E,\qquad \mathbf J=-ne\mathbf v_d=\frac{ne^2\tau}{m}\mathbf E\;\Longrightarrow\;\sigma=\frac{ne^2\tau}{m},\qquad \mu=\frac{e\tau}{m}$$ $$\frac{K}{\sigma T}=L\quad\text{(Wiedemann–Franz law, }L\text{ the Lorenz number)}$$

It gives Ohm's law from a microscopic picture, explains why metals conduct heat and electricity in proportion, and gets the order of magnitude of the conductivity right.

Failures — say all four

1 · Specific heat. Predicts an electronic contribution \(\frac32Nk_B\), i.e. a total \(\frac92R\) rather than the observed \(3R\) (Dulong–Petit). The measured electronic part is ~1% of this and is linear in \(T\).
2 · Paramagnetic susceptibility. Predicts \(\chi\propto1/T\); metals show a small, essentially temperature-independent susceptibility.
3 · Mean free path. To fit the conductivity, \(\lambda\) must be tens of interatomic spacings — impossible if electrons collide with every ion.
4 · Lorenz number. The computed \(L\) is about half the measured value, and the theory cannot say why some solids are insulators at all.

Unit 7

Sommerfeld's quantum theory: free electron gas in one dimension

You carry over

The particle in a box: \(\psi_n=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)\), \(E_n=n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2\).

New ground
  • Keep Drude's picture, replace the statistics: Fermi–Dirac and the Pauli principle
  • Filling levels two at a time, up to \(E_F\)
DeriveFermi energy of a 1D electron gas4 marks
Step 1 · the box\(N\) electrons in a line of length \(L\) with infinite walls: \(E_n=\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\dfrac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2\), \(n=1,2,3\dots\)
Step 2 · fill by PauliEach level holds two electrons (spin up and down). At \(T=0\) the electrons occupy the lowest levels, so the topmost filled level is $$n_F=\frac{N}{2}$$
Step 3 · the Fermi energy$$E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{n_F\pi}{L}\right)^2=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{N\pi}{2L}\right)^2$$ The Fermi energy is simply the energy of the highest occupied level at absolute zero.
Step 4 · density of states in 1DFrom \(N=2n=\dfrac{2L}{\pi}\dfrac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}\), $$D(E)=\frac{dN}{dE}=\frac{L}{\pi\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{2m}{E}}\ \propto\ E^{-1/2}$$ Note the sign of the exponent — in 1D the density of states falls with energy, unlike the 3D case.
Unit 8

Fermi energy, total energy, density of states, filling of levels

You carry over

The 1D result above, and the idea of counting states.

New ground

  • \(D(E)\propto\sqrt E\) and \(D(E_F)=3N/2E_F\)

DeriveFermi energy and density of states in three dimensions8 marks · the big one
Step 1 · states in k-spaceCube of side \(L\), periodic boundary conditions, \(\psi_{\mathbf k}=V^{-1/2}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\) with \(k_x=2\pi n_x/L\) etc. Each allowed state occupies a volume $$\Omega_k=\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^3=\frac{8\pi^3}{V}$$
Step 2 · fill the sphere\(E=\hbar^2k^2/2m\) depends only on \(|k|\), so at \(T=0\) the occupied states fill a sphere of radius \(k_F\). With two spins per state, $$N=2\cdot\frac{\frac43\pi k_F^3}{8\pi^3/V}=\frac{V}{3\pi^2}k_F^3$$
Step 3 · the Fermi parameters$$\boxed{k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}},\qquad \boxed{E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3}},\qquad v_F=\frac{\hbar k_F}{m},\qquad T_F=\frac{E_F}{k_B}$$ with \(n=N/V\). Everything depends only on the electron density.
Step 4 · density of statesInvert Step 2 to \(N(E)=\dfrac{V}{3\pi^2}\left(\dfrac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\) and differentiate: $$\boxed{D(E)=\frac{V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}E^{1/2}}\ \propto\ \sqrt E$$
Step 5 · two results that save time laterLogarithmic differentiation of \(N\propto E_F^{3/2}\) gives $$D(E_F)=\frac{3}{2}\frac{N}{E_F}$$ and averaging \(E\) over the filled sphere gives $$\bar E_0=\frac1N\int_0^{E_F}E\,D(E)\,dE=\frac{3}{5}E_F$$ so the electron gas has a huge zero-point energy: metals are under electronic pressure even at \(T=0\).
Fermi–Dirac distribution $$f(E)=\frac{1}{e^{(E-E_F)/k_BT}+1}$$

At \(T=0\) it is a step: 1 below \(E_F\), 0 above. At any \(T\), \(f(E_F)=\tfrac12\) exactly — that is the cleanest definition of \(E_F\). Raising \(T\) only rounds the step over a width \(\sim k_BT\); since \(E_F\sim5\) eV and \(k_BT\sim0.025\) eV at room temperature, only about 1% of the electrons are affected by heating at all. Everything below is frozen by the Pauli principle — there is nowhere for those electrons to go.

Unit 9

Applications of the free electron gas model

You carry over

Drude's \(\sigma=ne^2\tau/m\) and the failures listed in Unit 6.

New ground
  • Electronic specific heat linear in \(T\), and small
  • Why the mean free path can be huge
  • What the model still cannot explain
Electronic specific heat $$N_{\rm eff}\approx N\frac{k_BT}{E_F}=N\frac{T}{T_F}\;\Longrightarrow\;E_{\rm th}\approx Nk_B\frac{T^2}{T_F}\;\Longrightarrow\;C_v\approx\frac{3}{2}Nk_B\frac{T}{T_F}$$ $$\text{exact Sommerfeld result:}\qquad C_v=\frac{\pi^2}{2}Nk_B\frac{T}{T_F}=\gamma T$$

Only the electrons within \(k_BT\) of \(E_F\) have empty states to move into, so only a fraction \(T/T_F\) of them can absorb heat. With \(T_F\sim10^4\)–\(10^5\) K, at 300 K that fraction is about 1% — which both suppresses the magnitude and makes \(C_v\propto T\), exactly as measured. The total low-temperature heat capacity is \(C=\gamma T+AT^3\), electronic plus lattice.

  • Mean free path. A perfectly periodic lattice does not scatter an electron wave at all — resistance comes from deviations from periodicity (thermal vibrations, impurities, defects). That is why \(\lambda\) can be hundreds of atomic spacings, and why \(\rho\) falls as \(T\) falls.
  • Paramagnetism. Only the \(\sim T/T_F\) electrons near \(E_F\) can flip, giving the small, nearly \(T\)-independent Pauli susceptibility that is observed.
  • Wiedemann–Franz. The quantum calculation gives \(L=\dfrac{\pi^2}{3}\left(\dfrac{k_B}{e}\right)^2=2.45\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,\Omega\,K^{-2}}\), in good agreement with experiment.
  • Still unexplained: why some solids are insulators, the sign of the Hall coefficient in some metals, and the very idea of a band gap. For that you must put the ions back — Unit 10.
Unit 10

Band theory: Bloch's theorem, Kronig–Penney, velocity and effective mass

You carry over

The free electron gas, and (from quantum mechanics) the barrier problem and wave packets.

New ground
  • Bloch's theorem: what a wave function must look like in a periodic potential
  • The Kronig–Penney condition, and how bands and gaps appear from it
  • \(v=\frac1\hbar\frac{dE}{dk}\) and \(m^*=\hbar^2/(d^2E/dk^2)\), including negative mass
Bloch's theorem $$V(x+a)=V(x)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \psi_k(x)=e^{ikx}u_k(x),\quad u_k(x+a)=u_k(x)$$

A travelling plane wave modulated by a function with the periodicity of the lattice. Equivalently \(\psi(x+a)=e^{ika}\psi(x)\): moving one cell along multiplies the wave function by a phase, so \(|\psi|^2\) is periodic and the electron is not localised — a perfect lattice is transparent to it.

DeriveThe Kronig–Penney result and what it says8 marks
Step 1 · the model potentialAn array of square wells of width \(a\) and barriers of height \(V_0\), width \(b\), period \(a+b\). Define, for \(E<V_0\), $$\alpha^2=\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2},\qquad \beta^2=\frac{2m(V_0-E)}{\hbar^2}$$
Step 2 · Bloch form in each regionWrite \(\psi=e^{ikx}u_k(x)\) and solve for \(u\) in the well and in the barrier, then apply continuity of \(u\) and \(du/dx\) at \(x=0\) and, using periodicity, at \(x=a\) and \(x=-b\) — four conditions on four constants.
Step 3 · set the determinant to zero$$\frac{\beta^2-\alpha^2}{2\alpha\beta}\sinh\beta b\,\sin\alpha a+\cosh\beta b\,\cos\alpha a=\cos k(a+b)$$
Step 4 · the delta-barrier limitLet \(V_0\to\infty\), \(b\to0\) with \(V_0b\) finite. Then \(\sinh\beta b\to\beta b\), \(\cosh\beta b\to1\), and the whole thing collapses to $$\boxed{P\frac{\sin\alpha a}{\alpha a}+\cos\alpha a=\cos ka},\qquad P=\frac{mV_0ba}{\hbar^2}$$ \(P\) is the dimensionless barrier strength — how tightly the lattice binds the electron.
Step 5 · read off the physicsThe right-hand side is a cosine, so it can never leave \([-1,1]\). Wherever the left-hand function does leave that range, no real \(k\) exists — a forbidden gap. Where it stays inside, there is an allowed band. Four consequences: ① bands and gaps alternate; ② as \(\alpha a\) grows the \(\sin(\alpha a)/\alpha a\) term shrinks, so higher bands are wider and gaps narrower; ③ \(P\to\infty\) gives \(\sin\alpha a=0\), i.e. \(E_n=n^2h^2/8ma^2\) — isolated atomic levels; ④ \(P\to0\) gives \(\alpha=k\), the free electron \(E=\hbar^2k^2/2m\). The gaps open exactly at \(k=n\pi/a\), the Brillouin zone boundaries — where Unit 5 said Bragg reflection happens.
Velocity and effective mass $$v=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{dE}{dk},\qquad \hbar\frac{dk}{dt}=F_{\rm ext},\qquad \boxed{m^*=\frac{\hbar^2}{d^2E/dk^2}}$$

The electron responds to an external force as if it had mass \(m^*\); the difference from \(m\) is the lattice force, hidden inside \(E(k)\). Near the bottom of a band \(d^2E/dk^2>0\) and \(m^*>0\), and small for a sharply curved band. At the inflection point \(m^*\to\infty\) — the electron will not accelerate at all. Near the top \(d^2E/dk^2<0\) and \(m^*\) is negative: the lattice takes more momentum from the field than the electron gains, through Bragg reflection. A nearly full band is then better described by its few empty states, which behave as positive charges with positive mass — holes.

Unit 11

Distinction between metals, insulators and semiconductors

You carry over

The three-box picture from +2: overlapping bands, small gap, large gap.

New ground
  • The proof that a completely filled band carries no current
  • Band capacity \(2N\) states, and why divalent metals still conduct
  • \(\sigma\propto e^{-E_g/2k_BT}\) for a semiconductor
Capacity of a band

A band formed from one atomic level of \(N\) atoms contains \(N\) values of \(k\) in the first zone, hence \(2N\) states with spin. So a monovalent metal (Na, Cu) half-fills its band and conducts; a divalent element would exactly fill it and should be an insulator — unless the next band overlaps, which is exactly what happens in Mg, Ca and Zn.

Why a full band carries nothing $$N_{\rm eff}=\frac{2Lm}{\pi\hbar^2}\left.\frac{dE}{dk}\right|_{k=k_1}\qquad\xrightarrow{\ k_1=\pi/a\ }\qquad \frac{dE}{dk}=0\;\Longrightarrow\;N_{\rm eff}=0$$

For every electron moving one way there is one moving the other, and the field cannot change that because there are no empty states to move into. This is the single sentence that separates band theory from the free-electron model, and it is why insulators exist at all.

Trap

A semiconductor at \(T=0\) is an insulator — there is no difference of principle between them, only of gap size. And the temperature signs are opposite: a metal's conductivity falls with temperature (scattering wins), a semiconductor's rises (carrier creation wins). Getting that pair the wrong way round is an easy mark to lose.

Checkpoint · say it out loudSodium is monovalent and conducts. Magnesium is divalent, so its band should be exactly full — why does it still conduct?

A band from one atomic level of \(N\) atoms holds \(2N\) electrons. Sodium supplies \(N\), so the band is half full and there are empty states immediately above the Fermi level — a metal.

Magnesium supplies \(2N\), which would exactly fill the band and make it an insulator. It conducts because the next band overlaps the first in energy: electrons spill into the bottom of the upper band, leaving empty states at the top of the lower one. Both bands are then partly filled, so magnesium is a metal — and its conductivity involves both electrons and holes.

Langevin theory of dia- and paramagnetism; Weiss theory of ferromagnetism

DeriveLangevin theory of diamagnetism5 marks
Step 1 · switching on the fieldApplying \(\mathbf B\) changes the flux through an electron's orbit; by Lenz's law the induced change opposes it. Formally the orbit precesses about \(\mathbf B\) at the Larmor frequency $$\omega_L=\frac{eB}{2m}$$
Step 2 · precession is an extra currentThat precession is an additional circulation of charge: a current \(I=-\dfrac{e\omega_L}{2\pi}=-\dfrac{e^2B}{4\pi m}\).
Step 3 · its magnetic momentMultiplying by the mean area of the orbit projected perpendicular to \(\mathbf B\), \(\langle\rho^2\rangle=\frac23\langle r^2\rangle\) for a spherical charge cloud, $$\mu=IA=-\frac{e^2B}{6m}\langle r^2\rangle\quad\text{per electron}$$
Step 4 · sum and divideWith \(N\) atoms per unit volume and \(Z\) electrons each, \(M=N Z\mu\) and \(\chi=\mu_0M/B\): $$\boxed{\chi_{\rm dia}=-\frac{\mu_0NZe^2\langle r^2\rangle}{6m}}$$ Negative (it opposes the field) and with no \(T\) in it — matching experiment on both counts. DeriveLangevin theory of paramagnetism → the Curie law8 marks · near-certain
Step 1 · the competitionEach atom carries a permanent moment \(\mu\). The field wants to align it (\(U=-\mu B\cos\theta\)); thermal agitation randomises it. The equilibrium distribution is Boltzmann, \(\propto e^{\mu B\cos\theta/k_BT}\).
Step 2 · average the projectionAveraging \(\mu\cos\theta\) over solid angle with that weight, and writing \(a=\dfrac{\mu B}{k_BT}\): $$\langle\mu\cos\theta\rangle=\mu\frac{\int_0^\pi e^{a\cos\theta}\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta}{\int_0^\pi e^{a\cos\theta}\sin\theta\,d\theta}=\mu\left[\coth a-\frac1a\right]$$
Step 3 · the Langevin function$$M=N\mu\,L(a),\qquad L(a)=\coth a-\frac1a$$ For \(a\gg1\) (huge field or very low \(T\)), \(L\to1\) and \(M\to N\mu\): saturation, every moment aligned.
Step 4 · the ordinary limitAt room temperature in a laboratory field, \(a\sim10^{-3}\), and \(L(a)\approx a/3\). Susceptibility falls as \(1/T\) because heat is the only thing opposing alignment — plot \(1/\chi\) against \(T\) and you get a straight line through the origin.
DeriveWeiss theory of ferromagnetism → Curie–Weiss8 marks
Step 1 · the molecular fieldIron stays magnetised with no applied field, so something must be aligning the moments. Weiss postulated an internal molecular field proportional to the magnetisation itself: $$B_{\rm eff}=B+\lambda\mu_0M$$ (Its real origin is the quantum exchange interaction — a classical dipole field is a thousand times too weak.)
Step 2 · feed it into LangevinUse the small-argument result of the last ladder, but with \(B_{\rm eff}\) in place of \(B\): $$M=\frac{N\mu^2}{3k_BT}\left(B+\lambda\mu_0M\right)$$
Step 3 · solve for the susceptibilityCollect the \(M\) terms and write \(C=\mu_0N\mu^2/3k_B\), \(B=\mu_0H\): $$\chi=\frac{M}{H}=\frac{C}{T-\lambda C}\;\Longrightarrow\;\boxed{\chi=\frac{C}{T-\theta}},\qquad \theta=\lambda C$$ the Curie–Weiss law. Plotting \(1/\chi\) against \(T\) gives a straight line with intercept \(\theta\) on the \(T\)-axis — that intercept is the measurement of the molecular field.
Step 4 · below the Curie point\(\chi\to\infty\) at \(T=\theta\): the material can magnetise itself with no help. Below \(T_c\approx\theta\) a non-zero solution of \(M=N\mu L(\lambda\mu_0\mu M/k_BT)\) exists even at \(B=0\) — found graphically as the intersection of the Langevin curve with a straight line of slope \(\propto T\). Above \(T_c\) the only intersection is at the origin and the material becomes an ordinary paramagnet. For iron \(T_c=1043\) K.
Step 5 · why a lump of iron can be unmagnetisedSpontaneous magnetisation is only local: the crystal breaks into domains (typically \(10^{-6}\)–\(10^{-4}\) m) separated by Bloch walls, arranged so the net moment is nearly zero — it costs less energy than one big external field would. Applying a field first moves the walls (reversibly at first) and then rotates the domains, which is what produces the hysteresis loop.

Antiferromagnetism and ferrimagnetism

You carry over

Nothing — these two are new, and the syllabus asks only for qualitative ideas.

New ground

Antiferromagnetism $$\chi=\frac{C}{T+\theta}\quad(T>T_N)$$

Exchange coupling is negative, so neighbouring moments align antiparallel. Two interpenetrating sublattices of equal moment cancel exactly, giving no spontaneous magnetisation. Raising \(T\) first helps (it disrupts the cancelling order), so \(\chi\) rises to a maximum at the Néel temperature \(T_N\), above which the material is an ordinary paramagnet and \(\chi\) falls again. MnO (\(T_N=116\) K), Cr (308 K), FeO, NiO.

Ferrimagnetism

The same antiparallel arrangement, but the two sublattices have unequal moments, so they do not cancel and a large net magnetisation survives — behaving outwardly like a ferromagnet, with a Curie point and a hysteresis loop. The classic materials are the ferrites, MO·Fe₂O₃ (M = Mn, Ni, Zn, Fe), including magnetite Fe₃O₄, the original lodestone.

Why they matter: ferrites are ceramic and so have very high resistivity, which almost eliminates eddy-current losses. That makes them the standard core material for high-frequency transformers, inductors and antenna rods, where solid iron would be unusable.

Four sources of polarisability $$\alpha=\alpha_e+\alpha_i+\alpha_o+\alpha_s$$

Electronic \(\alpha_e\): the electron cloud shifts relative to the nucleus. Present in every material, \(\alpha_e=4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3\), and so fast it survives to optical frequencies (\(\sim10^{15}\) Hz).
Ionic \(\alpha_i\): positive and negative ions displace relative to each other. Ionic solids only; follows up to infrared (\(\sim10^{13}\) Hz).
Orientational \(\alpha_o=\mu^2/3k_BT\): permanent molecular dipoles turn towards the field (H₂O, HCl). The only temperature-dependent term — same \(1/3k_BT\) Langevin average as paramagnetism. Dies out by microwave frequencies (\(\sim10^{10}\)–\(10^{11}\) Hz).
Space-charge \(\alpha_s\): charges piling up at grain boundaries and interfaces; only at very low frequency.

Unit 22

Ferroelectricity and piezoelectricity

You carry over

Nothing — but the whole unit is the exact analogue of ferromagnetism, so borrow that structure.

New ground

Ferroelectricity

A spontaneous polarisation that can be reversed by an applied field. The signature is a \(P\)–\(E\) hysteresis loop with a remanent polarisation \(P_r\) and a coercive field \(E_c\), exactly parallel to the magnetic case — including domains. Above the ferroelectric Curie temperature the spontaneous polarisation vanishes and the material becomes an ordinary (paraelectric) dielectric obeying $$\varepsilon_r\propto\frac{C}{T-T_c}$$ Examples: barium titanate BaTiO₃ (\(T_c=120^\circ\)C), Rochelle salt, KDP. \(\varepsilon_r\) can reach several thousand near \(T_c\) — which is why BaTiO₃ is in almost every ceramic capacitor. Uses: capacitors, non-volatile memory, infrared detection (pyroelectricity).

Piezoelectricity

Direct effect: mechanical stress produces a polarisation, hence a measurable voltage. Converse effect: an applied field produces a strain. Both require a crystal with no centre of symmetry — otherwise stress cannot separate the charge centres. Examples: quartz, Rochelle salt, tourmaline, PZT.

Uses: quartz oscillators and watches, ultrasonic transducers, microphones and pickups, gas-lighter sparkers, accelerometers, inkjet heads. Remember the one-way implication: every ferroelectric is piezoelectric, but most piezoelectrics — quartz among them — are not ferroelectric.

CheckpointA dielectric has \(\varepsilon_r=4.0\) and \(N=3\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\). Find the polarizability \(\alpha\).

Clausius–Mossotti: \(\dfrac{\varepsilon_r-1}{\varepsilon_r+2}=\dfrac{N\alpha}{3\varepsilon_0}\).

Left side \(=\dfrac{3}{6}=0.5\).

\(\alpha=\dfrac{3\varepsilon_0\times0.5}{N}=\dfrac{3\times8.85\times10^{-12}\times0.5}{3\times10^{28}}=\) \(4.4\times10^{-40}\ \mathrm{F\,m^2}\).

Sanity check with \(\alpha_e=4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3\): that \(\alpha\) corresponds to \(R\approx1.5\) Å, a plausible atomic radius — always worth one line.

The bridges between micro and macro

Density ↔ lattice constant $$\rho=\frac{N\,M}{N_A\,a^3}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad a=\left(\frac{N\,M}{N_A\,\rho}\right)^{1/3}$$

\(N\) = atoms per unit cell (1, 2, 4, 8), \(M\) = atomic weight in kg/mol, \(\rho\) in kg/m³. This single relation converts a measured density into a lattice constant and back, and it appears in Module I numericals every year. For a compound like NaCl, \(N\) is the number of formula units and \(M\) the molecular weight.

Electron concentration in a metal $$n=\frac{\rho\,N_A\,z}{M}$$

\(z\) = valence electrons per atom. This is the \(n\) that goes into \(k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}\) and \(\sigma=ne^2\tau/m\). For copper, \(n=8.5\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\) and \(E_F=7.0\) eV — the numbers to carry as a sanity check.

Moves you are expected to make without comment

Take logs of an exponential

\(\sigma=\sigma_0e^{-E_g/2k_BT}\Rightarrow\ln\sigma\) vs \(1/T\) is a straight line of slope \(-E_g/2k_B\). Same move for \(\chi\): plot \(1/\chi\) vs \(T\) and read the intercept.

Small-argument Langevin

\(L(a)=\coth a-1/a\approx a/3\) for \(a\ll1\). This one approximation produces both the Curie law and the orientational polarisability \(\mu^2/3k_BT\).

Count shared atoms

Corner \(\tfrac18\), face \(\tfrac12\), edge \(\tfrac14\), interior 1. Applies to atoms, ions and lattice points alike.

Find the touching direction first

Every packing-fraction question is: which direction do the spheres touch along, and how many radii fit into it? Edge for SC, face diagonal for FCC, body diagonal for BCC.

Check with a limit

Does \(P\to0\) give the free electron? Does \(T\to\infty\) give \(\chi\to0\)? Does \(N\alpha\to0\) give \(\varepsilon_r\to1\)? One line, and it catches sign errors.

Watch the factor of 2

\(E_g/2k_BT\) for intrinsic conductivity, \(2d\sin\theta\) for Bragg, \(2N\) states in a band, two electrons per level. Each of these has a companion formula without the 2.

Ten traps that eat marks

8:30 – 10:00
  1. In Bragg's law \(\theta\) is measured from the plane, not from the normal — and the formula has \(2d\), not \(d\).
  2. CsCl is simple cubic with a two-ion basis, not BCC; diamond is FCC with a two-atom basis, not a lattice of its own.
  3. Packing fraction needs the touching direction first: edge (SC), face diagonal (FCC), body diagonal (BCC).
  4. Reciprocal of BCC is FCC and of FCC is BCC — a two-mark gift if you remember it, a lost mark if you don't.
  5. \(E_F\) is defined by \(f(E_F)=\tfrac12\) at any temperature; the step picture is only the \(T=0\) case.
  6. Electronic specific heat is linear in \(T\) and about 1% of the classical value — quoting \(\frac32Nk_B\) is Drude's error.
  7. Negative effective mass is not a negative electron: it means the lattice is taking momentum via Bragg reflection.
  8. Intrinsic conductivity carries \(E_g/\mathbf{2}k_BT\) in the exponent — half the gap, because a pair is created each time.
  9. Metal conductivity falls with temperature; semiconductor conductivity rises. Opposite signs, same paper.
  10. The \(1/\chi\) intercept identifies the class: 0 para, \(+\theta\) ferro, \(-\theta\) antiferro. Say which, and draw it.
