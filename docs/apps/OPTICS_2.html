Module III · 10 hours · 15 marks · Hg 5461 Å

Diffraction

Two halves that are genuinely different. Fraunhofer (source and screen effectively at infinity) is a Fourier problem you can solve exactly. Fresnel (finite distances) is a phasor-spiral problem you solve by half-period zones.

Unit 12

Single-slit Fraunhofer diffraction

You carry over

Minima at \(b\sin\theta=m\lambda\); central maximum twice as wide as the others.

New ground
  • The full intensity \(I=I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2\), derived by integrating over the slit
  • Secondary maxima from \(\tan\beta=\beta\), and how weak they are (4.7%, 1.65%)
  • Fraunhofer vs Fresnel as a statement about wavefront curvature
DeriveSingle-slit intensity6 marks · asked most years
Step 1 · chop the slitSlit of width \(b\), split into \(N\) equal strips. Adjacent strips differ in path by \(\frac{b}{N}\sin\theta\), i.e. in phase by \(\Delta=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\frac{b}{N}\sin\theta\). All \(N\) phasors are equal in length and turn by the same step: the phasor polygon is an arc of a circle.
Step 2 · total turnAcross the whole slit the phase runs through $$2\beta=\frac{2\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \beta=\frac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda}$$
Step 3 · chord over arcAn arc of length \(A_0\) bent through \(2\beta\) has radius \(A_0/2\beta\) and chord \(2\cdot\frac{A_0}{2\beta}\sin\beta\). So $$A=A_0\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{I=I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^{2}}$$ (The same result follows from \(\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}e^{ikx\sin\theta}dx\) if you prefer the integral.)
Step 4 · minima\(I=0\) when \(\sin\beta=0\) but \(\beta\ne0\): \(\beta=m\pi\), i.e. $$b\sin\theta=m\lambda,\quad m=\pm1,\pm2,\dots$$ \(m=0\) is excluded — there \(\sin\beta/\beta\to1\), the central maximum.
Step 5 · secondary maxima\(dI/d\beta=0\) gives \(\tan\beta=\beta\), whose roots are \(\beta=1.430\pi,\,2.459\pi,\,3.471\pi\) — not half-integers. Relative intensities: $$1,\;0.0472,\;0.0165,\;0.0083$$ The first side lobe carries under 5% of the centre; that is why the pattern looks like one bright band.
Step 6 · widthAngular half-width \(\sin\theta_1=\lambda/b\); the central maximum spans \(2\lambda/b\) in angle and \(2f\lambda/b\) on a screen at the focal plane of a lens of focal length \(f\). Narrower slit ⇒ wider pattern.

Fraunhofer or Fresnel? Compare the Fresnel number \(F=b^2/\lambda D\). If \(F\ll1\) the wavefronts reaching the screen are effectively plane — Fraunhofer, and a lens can always create this condition at its focal plane. If \(F\gtrsim1\) the curvature matters — Fresnel (Units 15–16).

Unit 13

Two-slit Fraunhofer pattern and missing orders

You carry over

Young's fringes \(d\sin\theta=m\lambda\) — treating the slits as points.

New ground
  • Real slits have width, so interference fringes sit inside a diffraction envelope
  • The product formula \(I=4I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2\cos^2\gamma\)
  • Missing orders: \(d/b = m/p\)
Two-slit intensity $$I=4I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\gamma,\qquad \beta=\frac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda},\quad \gamma=\frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}$$

Two factors, two physical origins. \((\sin\beta/\beta)^2\) is diffraction by each slit of width \(b\) — the slowly varying envelope. \(\cos^2\gamma\) is interference between the two slits separated by \(d\) — the rapid fringes. \(d\gt b\) always, so the fringes are always finer than the envelope.

Missing orders $$\frac{d}{b}=\frac{m}{p}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{order } m \text{ is missing}$$

When an interference maximum \(d\sin\theta=m\lambda\) lands exactly on a diffraction minimum \(b\sin\theta=p\lambda\), the envelope is zero there and the fringe disappears. Example: \(d=3b\) ⇒ orders 3, 6, 9… are missing. If \(d=2b\), the even orders vanish.

Unit 14

N-slit Fraunhofer pattern and the plane grating

You carry over

A grating splits light into spectra; \(d\sin\theta=m\lambda\).

New ground
  • The full \(\left(\frac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma}\right)^2\) factor, and why maxima sharpen as \(N\) grows
  • \(N-1\) minima and \(N-2\) secondary maxima between principal maxima
  • Angular dispersion and resolving power \(=mN\)
N-slit intensity $$I=I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma}\right)^{2}$$

Principal maxima: \(\gamma=m\pi\), i.e. \(d\sin\theta=m\lambda\); there both \(\sin N\gamma\) and \(\sin\gamma\) vanish and the limit is \(N^2\) — intensity grows as \(N^2\), not \(N\). Minima: \(N\gamma=p\pi\) with \(p\ne mN\), giving \(N-1\) zeros and therefore \(N-2\) faint secondary maxima between neighbours. Half-width of a principal maximum \(\propto 1/N\): more lines ⇒ sharper lines. That single fact is the whole reason gratings beat prisms.

Grating: the four working formulas $$(e+b)\sin\theta=m\lambda,\qquad d=e+b=\frac{1}{N_{\rm lines/cm}}$$ $$m_{\max}=\left\lfloor \frac{d}{\lambda}\right\rfloor,\qquad \frac{d\theta}{d\lambda}=\frac{m}{d\cos\theta},\qquad \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda}=mN$$

\(N\) in the resolving power is the total number of lines illuminated, not lines per cm. Rayleigh's criterion: two lines are just resolved when the maximum of one falls on the first minimum of the other. Oblique incidence generalises the first formula to \(d(\sin i\pm\sin\theta)=m\lambda\).

Trap

\(b\sin\theta=m\lambda\) is a minimum (single slit). \(d\sin\theta=m\lambda\) is a maximum (grating). Identical shape, opposite meaning — always say which quantity is the slit width and which is the grating element before you use either.

CheckpointA grating has 5000 lines/cm and 2 cm is illuminated. In the second order, can it resolve the sodium doublet (5890 and 5896 Å)?

Total lines \(N=5000\times2=10^4\). Resolving power \(=mN=2\times10^4\). Required \(=\lambda/\Delta\lambda = 5893/6 \approx 982\). Since \(20000 \gg 982\), yes, comfortably — it would resolve even in first order (\(10^4 > 982\)).
Unit 15

Fresnel diffraction: half-period zones and the zone plate

You carry over

Huygens' principle: every point of a wavefront is a secondary source.

New ground
  • Dividing the wavefront into half-period zones of equal area
  • The startling result \(A=A_1/2\): the whole wave does a quarter of what the first zone alone would
  • The zone plate — a lens made only of opaque rings, with multiple foci
DeriveHalf-period zones and \(A=A_1/2\)6 marks
Step 1 · constructionPlane wavefront, observation point \(P\) at distance \(b\). Draw spheres about \(P\) of radii \(b+\lambda/2,\,b+2\lambda/2,\dots\) The rings they cut on the wavefront are the half-period zones: successive zones differ in path to \(P\) by \(\lambda/2\), hence in phase by \(\pi\).
Step 2 · radii and areas$$r_n^2=\left(b+\frac{n\lambda}{2}\right)^2-b^2\approx n\lambda b\;\Longrightarrow\; r_n=\sqrt{n\lambda b}$$ Area of the \(n\)th zone \(=\pi(r_n^2-r_{n-1}^2)=\pi\lambda b\) — the same for every zone.
Step 3 · the amplitudesEqual areas, but the distance to \(P\) grows and the obliquity factor falls, so \(A_1\gt A_2\gt A_3\dots\), slowly and monotonically. Successive zones are \(\pi\) out of phase, so the total alternates: $$A=A_1-A_2+A_3-A_4+\cdots$$
Step 4 · sum the alternating seriesGroup as \(A=\frac{A_1}{2}+\left(\frac{A_1}{2}-A_2+\frac{A_3}{2}\right)+\cdots\). Because the decrease is slow and steady, each bracket \(\approx0\), and the last term \(\to0\). So $$\boxed{A=\frac{A_1}{2}}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad I=\frac{I_1}{4}$$ The entire wavefront delivers a quarter of the intensity the first zone would deliver on its own. Rectilinear propagation is what is left after almost everything cancels.
Step 5 · so block the even zonesIf alternate zones are made opaque, nothing cancels: \(A=A_1+A_3+A_5+\cdots\), an enormous amplitude at \(P\). That is a zone plate — a lens with no glass.
Zone plate $$r_n^2=n\lambda f\;\Longrightarrow\; f_1=\frac{r_n^2}{n\lambda};\qquad \frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}=\frac{n\lambda}{r_n^2}$$

Subsidiary foci at \(f_1/3,\,f_1/5,\,f_1/7,\dots\) (odd denominators) — a real lens has one. And \(f\propto1/\lambda\): red focuses nearer, violet farther, the opposite of a glass lens, so its chromatic aberration has the opposite sign. Positive (transparent odd zones) and negative zone plates are complementary.

Unit 16

Diffraction by a straight edge

You carry over

"Shadows have fuzzy edges."

New ground
  • The Cornu spiral as the phasor sum of a continuously exposed wavefront
  • Unequally spaced bright and dark bands outside the geometric shadow
  • Exactly \(I_0/4\) at the geometric edge; a smooth monotonic decay inside the shadow

An opaque edge exposes half the wavefront plus a variable strip. Adding the strips as phasors traces the Cornu spiral, whose coordinates are the Fresnel integrals \(C(v)=\int_0^v\cos\frac{\pi t^2}{2}dt\) and \(S(v)=\int_0^v\sin\frac{\pi t^2}{2}dt\), with

Straight edge $$v=x\sqrt{\frac{2(a+b)}{\lambda ab}},\qquad I(v)=\frac{I_0}{2}\left[\left(\tfrac12+C(v)\right)^2+\left(\tfrac12+S(v)\right)^2\right]$$

The chord from the spiral's lower eye \((-\frac12,-\frac12)\) to the point \(v\) is the amplitude. At \(v\to\infty\) the chord is the full diagonal ⇒ \(I=I_0\). At the geometric edge \(v=0\) the chord is half the diagonal ⇒ \(I=I_0/4\). Inside the shadow (\(v\lt0\)) the chord shortens monotonically — no fringes in the shadow. In the lit region the chord winds around the upper eye, giving bands of decreasing width and contrast.

$$\text{maxima at } v^2=4n-\tfrac52,\qquad \text{minima at } v^2=4n-\tfrac12\qquad (n=1,2,3\dots)$$

First maximum at \(v=1.22\) with \(I\approx1.37\,I_0\) — brighter than with no obstacle at all. Successive bands crowd together because \(x_n\propto v_n\propto\sqrt{n}\), which is also why a straight-edge pattern looks nothing like a grating's evenly spaced fringes.

Unit 17

Polarisation — the states of light

You carry over

Light is a transverse EM wave; polaroids block one direction; Brewster's law \(\tan i_p=n\).

New ground
  • Five states, not two: unpolarised, linear, circular, elliptical, partial
  • Degree of polarisation \(P\)
  • The ellipse equation from two perpendicular SHMs — the algebra behind the whole module

Polarisation exists because light is transverse: longitudinal waves (sound) cannot be polarised, so any polarisation effect is direct proof of transversality. The state is fixed by how the tip of \(\mathbf E\) moves in the plane perpendicular to propagation. Superpose two perpendicular components of the same frequency, \(x=a\sin\omega t\) and \(y=b\sin(\omega t+\delta)\), eliminate \(t\):

The master equation of Module IV $$\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}-\frac{2xy}{ab}\cos\delta=\sin^2\delta$$

One equation, every state:

  • \(\delta=0\) or \(\pi\) ⇒ \(y=\pm(b/a)x\): a straight line — plane polarised
  • \(\delta=\pi/2\) with \(a=b\) ⇒ \(x^2+y^2=a^2\): a circle — circularly polarised
  • \(\delta=\pi/2\) with \(a\ne b\) ⇒ ellipse with axes along \(x,y\)
  • any other \(\delta\) ⇒ a tilted ellipse
Degree of polarisation$$P=\frac{I_{\max}-I_{\min}}{I_{\max}+I_{\min}}$$

\(P=1\) plane polarised, \(P=0\) unpolarised or circular, \(0\lt P\lt1\) partially polarised. Note that \(P\) alone cannot tell circular from unpolarised — the reason Unit 22's flowchart needs a quarter-wave plate.

Unit 18

Production of linearly polarised light

You carry over

Brewster's law and polaroids, as facts.

New ground
  • Why \(i_p+r=90^\circ\) follows from Brewster's law
  • The pile of plates and the Nicol prism, with numbers
  • Dichroism vs double refraction as two different mechanisms
MethodMechanism & key relation
ReflectionAt \(\tan i_p=n\) the reflected beam is completely plane polarised, vibrations ⊥ plane of incidence. Since \(\tan i_p = \sin i_p/\cos i_p = \sin i_p/\sin r\) requires \(\cos i_p = \sin r\), we get \(i_p+r=90^\circ\): reflected ⊥ refracted. For glass \(i_p\approx57^\circ\). Only ~8% of the incident light is reflected, so it is polarised but faint.
Refraction
(pile of plates)		Each surface removes a little of the ⊥ component. Stack 15–20 glass plates at \(i_p\) and the transmitted beam becomes nearly completely polarised, with vibrations in the plane of incidence — perpendicular to the reflected beam's.
Dichroism
(Polaroid)		Aligned long-chain molecules (herapathite / stretched PVA + iodine) absorb one component. Cheap, large aperture, works over the whole visible band; but it wastes 50% of unpolarised light as heat.
Double refraction
(Nicol prism)		Separate O and E, then kill one. See Unit 20.
ScatteringLight scattered at \(90^\circ\) from small particles is completely plane polarised — why the blue sky is polarised in a band \(90^\circ\) from the sun.
Nicol prism — the numbers that matter

A calcite rhomb is cut so the end faces make \(68^\circ\) with the long edge (ground down from the natural \(71^\circ\)), sliced along the short diagonal and re-cemented with Canada balsam, \(n=1.55\). Balsam sits between the two calcite indices: \(n_e=1.486 \lt 1.55 \lt n_o=1.658\). So for the O-ray the balsam is a rarer medium and, at the design angle, the O-ray is totally internally reflected to the blackened side; the E-ray passes. Output: plane polarised, vibrations in the principal section.

Limitations (a favourite 2-marker): the field of view is only about \(14^\circ\)–\(30^\circ\) — too convergent and the O-ray stops being totally reflected, too divergent and the E-ray starts to be; the length must be \(\approx3\times\) the width; and Canada balsam absorbs ultraviolet.

Unit 19

Polariser and analyser: Malus's law

You carry over

\(I=I_0\cos^2\theta\), stated.

New ground
  • Its one-line derivation from the resolved amplitude
  • The \(I_0/2\) rule for unpolarised light entering the first polariser
  • The three-polariser paradox — light through crossed polarisers
DeriveMalus's law3 marks
Step 1 · resolvePlane polarised light of amplitude \(A\) meets an analyser whose transmission axis makes \(\theta\) with the vibration direction. Resolve: \(A\cos\theta\) along the axis (transmitted) and \(A\sin\theta\) across it (blocked).
Step 2 · square it$$I=kA^2\cos^2\theta=I_0\cos^2\theta$$ Extinction twice per rotation, at \(\theta=90^\circ\) and \(270^\circ\).
Step 3 · the unpolarised caseUnpolarised light has all \(\theta\) equally likely; \(\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle=\tfrac12\), so the first polariser always transmits \(I_0/2\) whatever its orientation. Rotating a single polaroid in unpolarised light shows no variation — the fact the whole of Unit 22 is built on.
Unit 20

Double refraction — Huygens' explanation

You carry over

Huygens' construction for a plane wave crossing a boundary.

New ground
  • Two refracted rays, only one of which obeys Snell's law
  • Two secondary wavelets from every point: a sphere (O) and an ellipsoid (E)
  • Optic axis, principal section, positive vs negative crystals

A calcite rhomb splits an incident beam into an ordinary (O) and an extraordinary (E) ray. Rotating the crystal about the beam makes the E image revolve around the fixed O image.

Ordinary rayExtraordinary ray
Snell's lawobeys it, \(n_o\) constantdoes not in general; \(n_e\) depends on direction
Waveletsphereellipsoid of revolution about the optic axis
Vibrations⊥ to the principal sectionin the principal section
At normal incidencegoes straight throughgenerally deviates — even at normal incidence
Along the optic axisboth travel at the same speed; no double refraction
Vocabulary you must define correctly

Optic axis: the direction (not a line) in the crystal along which O and E travel with equal speed — in calcite, the direction making equal angles with the three faces at a blunt corner. Principal section: the plane containing the optic axis and normal to a crystal face. Uniaxial crystals have one such direction (calcite, quartz); biaxial have two.

Negative crystal (calcite): \(n_e\lt n_o\) (1.486 < 1.658), so E travels faster and its ellipsoid lies outside the sphere, touching along the optic axis. Positive crystal (quartz): \(n_e\gt n_o\) (1.553 > 1.544), ellipsoid inside the sphere.

Huygens' construction, in the order you should draw it: ① mark the optic axis direction; ② from the point where the wavefront first meets the surface, draw a sphere (radius \(\propto1/n_o\)) and an ellipsoid (semi-axes \(\propto1/n_o\) and \(1/n_e\)) touching along the optic axis; ③ from the point where the wavefront last touches the surface, draw the tangent planes to sphere and to ellipsoid; ④ the two contact points give the O and E ray directions. The tangent to the ellipsoid is not perpendicular to the ray — which is exactly why the E-ray disobeys Snell.

Unit 21

Wave plates

You carry over

Nothing.

New ground
  • Retardation \(\delta=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(n_o-n_e)t\)
  • Quarter-wave and half-wave plate thicknesses
  • What each does to light at \(0^\circ,\,45^\circ,\,\theta\)

Cut the crystal with the optic axis parallel to the surface. Light at normal incidence then travels as O and E in the same direction but at different speeds, so they emerge with a phase difference.

Wave plates $$\delta=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\left(n_o-n_e\right)t$$ $$\text{QWP: } t=\frac{\lambda}{4\left(n_o-n_e\right)}\qquad \text{HWP: } t=\frac{\lambda}{2\left(n_o-n_e\right)}$$

Use \(|n_o-n_e|\): for calcite (negative) that is \(n_o-n_e\); for quartz (positive) it is \(n_e-n_o\). A QWP for sodium light in calcite is only about 0.86 µm thick, so real plates are made of mica or stacked quartz.

Trap

A half-wave plate rotates plane polarised light by \(2\theta\); it does not "reverse" it. And a QWP converts circular → plane — that single property is what makes it the test instrument in Unit 22.

Unit 22

Production and analysis of polarised light

You carry over

Nothing — and this is the single most predictable question on the Polarisation module.

New ground
  • How to produce each of the five states
  • The two-stage analysis flowchart: analyser alone, then QWP + analyser
  • Optical activity and specific rotation

Production

  • Plane: Nicol / polaroid / reflection at \(i_p\).
  • Circular: plane polarised light onto a QWP with the vibration at 45° to the plate's axis (equal amplitudes, \(\delta=\pi/2\)).
  • Elliptical: the same, but at any angle other than 0°, 45°, 90° (unequal amplitudes, \(\delta=\pi/2\)); or any \(\delta\ne0,\pi\).
  • Partially polarised: reflection at any angle other than \(i_p\); a single glass plate in transmission.

Analysis — the flowchart to reproduce in the exam

Stage 1 uses a rotating analyser alone. It always ends in one of three verdicts, and two of them are ambiguous — that ambiguity is the whole point of stage 2.

What the analyser alone showsCould beThen insert a QWP before the analyser and rotate again
Complete extinction twice per rotationPlane polarised— no further test needed
No variation at allUnpolarised or circularExtinction now ⇒ circular. Still no variation ⇒ unpolarised.
Variation, but never zeroPartially polarised or ellipticalExtinction now ⇒ elliptical. Still varies without extinction ⇒ partially polarised.

Why the QWP works. Circular light is two perpendicular components with \(\delta=\pi/2\); the QWP adds another \(\pi/2\), making \(\delta=\pi\) — a straight line, which an analyser can extinguish. It does nothing coherent to unpolarised light, whose components have no fixed phase relation at all. For elliptical light, the QWP axis must be aligned with the ellipse's major axis (found from the analyser's maximum) before extinction appears.

Optical activity

Specific rotation $$S=\frac{10\,\theta}{l\,c}$$

\(\theta\) in degrees, \(l\) the tube length in cm, \(c\) the concentration in g/cc. Quartz, sugar solution and turpentine rotate the plane of vibration; dextro-rotatory (right, clockwise looking towards the source) or laevo-rotatory. Measured with a Laurent half-shade polarimeter, whose half-wave quartz plate covers half the field so the eye matches two brightnesses instead of judging one minimum — far more sensitive.

Fresnel's explanation: plane polarised light is the sum of left- and right-circular components. In an active medium these travel at different speeds (\(n_L\ne n_R\)); recombining them gives a plane vibration rotated by \(\theta=\dfrac{\pi l\,(n_L-n_R)}{\lambda}\).

Checkpoint · the money questionA beam shows a maximum and a non-zero minimum as the analyser rotates. What are the two possibilities, and what single test separates them?

Partially polarised or elliptically polarised. Insert a quarter-wave plate before the analyser, with its optic axis parallel to the direction that gave maximum intensity. Elliptical light becomes plane polarised, so the analyser now gives complete extinction at some setting; partially polarised light is unaffected and still varies without ever going dark.

Five traps that eat marks (Modules III & IV)

Traps
  1. \(b\sin\theta=m\lambda\) is a minimum (slit); \(d\sin\theta=m\lambda\) is a maximum (grating). Say which is which.
  2. Grating resolving power uses \(N\) = total lines illuminated, not lines per cm.
  3. A zone plate has many foci and \(f\propto1/\lambda\) — chromatic behaviour opposite to a lens.
  4. Circular and unpolarised light both give no variation with an analyser alone. You must bring in a quarter-wave plate.
  5. In the analysis flowchart: check with analyser alone first, then insert QWP before analyser with its axis along maximum intensity.
