The one convention used everywhere here

Every wave function in this app is drawn with gold where \(\psi\) is positive and cyan where it is negative, with \(|\psi|^2\) shaded underneath. Sign is not decoration — it is what makes nodes, parity and orthogonality visible, and it is the first thing to draw in any exam sketch.

Module I · 6 hours · 10 marks · Griffiths §1.1–1.6

The Wave Function

Five short units that install the whole apparatus: a complex state, a law of motion for it, a rule for turning it into predictions, and the statistics you need to state those predictions properly.

Unit 1

The Schrödinger equation and the statistical interpretation

You carry over

\(\lambda=h/p\), \(E=h\nu\), Bohr's quantised orbits, "electrons behave like waves too". All of it handed to you as results, with no equation behind them.

New ground
  • The state is a complex function \(\Psi(x,t)\), not a trajectory
  • One equation of motion, playing the role of \(F=ma\)
  • Born's rule: \(|\Psi|^2\) is a probability density
  • Indeterminacy, the three positions on it, and collapse

Classically, \(m\ddot x=-\partial V/\partial x\) plus two initial conditions gives \(x(t)\), and every other quantity follows from it. Quantum mechanics replaces the trajectory with a wave function and Newton's law with:

Time-dependent Schrödinger equation $$i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2}+V(x,t)\,\Psi$$

\(\hbar=h/2\pi=1.0546\times10^{-34}\,\)J·s. Given \(\Psi(x,0)\), this determines \(\Psi(x,t)\) for all later \(t\) — deterministically. The indeterminacy of quantum mechanics is not in this equation; it enters only when you measure.

Born's statistical interpretation $$P_{ab}(t)=\int_a^b|\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx,\qquad |\Psi|^2=\Psi^*\Psi$$

\(|\Psi(x,t)|^2\) is the probability per unit length of finding the particle at \(x\). The probability of finding it at one exact point is zero — you must always integrate over an interval.

Trap

\(\Psi\) itself is not a probability and is not measurable — it is complex. \(|\Psi|^2\) is a density, with units of 1/length in 1D, so it can be greater than 1. Only its integral over a region is a probability.

The measurement problem — the 3-mark bookwork

A measurement finds the particle at \(x=C\). Where was it just before?

  • Realist: it was at \(C\); \(\Psi\) is an incomplete description and some hidden variable fixes the answer.
  • Orthodox (Copenhagen): it was nowhere definite; the measurement made it be at \(C\).
  • Agnostic: the question is unanswerable and therefore not physics.

Bell (1964) showed local realist theories obey inequalities that quantum mechanics violates; experiment sides with quantum mechanics, ruling out local hidden variables and leaving the orthodox position (or explicitly non-local theories such as de Broglie–Bohm).

Collapse — two kinds of evolution

A repeated measurement must give \(C\) again, so the first measurement must have changed the state into a spike at \(C\). Quantum mechanics therefore has two dynamics: continuous, deterministic, unitary evolution under the Schrödinger equation between measurements, and discontinuous, non-unitary collapse at a measurement.

Checkpoint · say it out loudWhy can't \(|\Psi(x,t)|^2\) be called "the probability that the particle is at \(x\)"?

Because \(x\) is a continuous variable: the probability of any single exact value is zero. \(|\Psi|^2\) is a probability density — probability per unit length — and only \(\int_a^b|\Psi|^2dx\) is a probability. Its dimensions are \(\mathrm{length}^{-1}\), which is also why \(|\Psi|^2\) may exceed 1 while a probability never can.
Unit 2

Probability: discrete and continuous variables

You carry over

Mean, median and mode; reading a histogram. Nothing here is difficult — it is the vocabulary the rest of the course is written in.

New ground
  • \(\langle f(j)\rangle\) for an arbitrary function, not just \(\langle j\rangle\)
  • Variance as \(\langle j^2\rangle-\langle j\rangle^2\), and the proof
  • Why \(\langle j^2\rangle\ge\langle j\rangle^2\) always
  • The continuous version, and why a density is not a probability
Discrete $$P(j)=\frac{N(j)}{N},\qquad \sum_j P(j)=1,\qquad \langle j\rangle=\sum_j jP(j),\qquad \langle f(j)\rangle=\sum_j f(j)P(j)$$

Note carefully: \(\langle j^2\rangle=\sum j^2P(j)\), which is not \(\langle j\rangle^2\). Confusing the two is the single most common slip in this module.

DeriveThe variance identity2–3 marks
Step 1 · why not just average the deviationLet \(\Delta j=j-\langle j\rangle\). Then \(\langle\Delta j\rangle=\langle j\rangle-\langle j\rangle=0\) always — the deviations cancel, so the mean deviation measures nothing. Square first.
Step 2 · expand the square$$\sigma^2\equiv\langle(\Delta j)^2\rangle=\big\langle j^2-2j\langle j\rangle+\langle j\rangle^2\big\rangle$$ \(\langle j\rangle\) is a number, so it comes out of the averages.
Step 3 · collect$$\sigma^2=\langle j^2\rangle-2\langle j\rangle\langle j\rangle+\langle j\rangle^2=\boxed{\langle j^2\rangle-\langle j\rangle^2}$$
Step 4 · the inequality for free\(\sigma^2\ge0\) because it is an average of squares, hence $$\langle j^2\rangle\ge\langle j\rangle^2$$ with equality only for a distribution of zero spread — every member the same. This is the seed of every uncertainty relation you will meet.
Continuous $$dP=\rho(x)\,dx,\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho\,dx=1,\qquad \langle x\rangle=\int x\rho\,dx,\qquad \sigma^2=\langle x^2\rangle-\langle x\rangle^2$$

Every sum becomes an integral and \(P(j)\) becomes \(\rho(x)dx\). In quantum mechanics \(\rho(x)=|\Psi(x,t)|^2\) — that substitution is the whole of Module I.

Unit 3

Normalization

You carry over

That a probability distribution must sum to 1, and how to do a definite integral.

New ground
  • Normalisation fixes \(|A|\) and never the phase
  • Square-integrability as the admission ticket for a physical state
  • The proof that normalisation, once imposed, stays — which needs the Schrödinger equation
Normalisation condition $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Psi(x,t)|^2dx=1$$

The Schrödinger equation is linear and homogeneous, so if \(\Psi\) solves it so does \(A\Psi\). That freedom is exactly what you spend on normalisation. It determines \(|A|\); the phase \(e^{i\theta}\) survives untouched and carries no physical content. States with \(\int|\Psi|^2=\infty\) (a plane wave, for one) cannot be normalised and are not physical states on their own.

DeriveNormalisation is preserved in time5 marks · classic
Step 1 · move the derivative inside$$\frac{d}{dt}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Psi|^2dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi|^2dx$$ (Ordinary derivative outside because the integral is a function of \(t\) alone.)
Step 2 · product rule$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi|^2=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\Psi^*\Psi)=\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t}\Psi$$
Step 3 · use the equation, and its conjugate$$\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2}-\frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi,\qquad \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t}=-\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi^*}{\partial x^2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi^*$$ The \(V\) terms cancel in the sum — this step needs \(V\) real.
Step 4 · recognise a total derivative$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi|^2=\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2}-\frac{\partial^2\Psi^*}{\partial x^2}\Psi\right)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\Psi\right)\right]$$
Step 5 · integrate and kill the boundary$$\frac{d}{dt}\int|\Psi|^2dx=\left[\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\Psi\right)\right]_{-\infty}^{+\infty}=0$$ because a normalisable \(\Psi\) and its derivative must vanish at \(\pm\infty\). Normalise once and it stays normalised. The bracket you just discarded is the probability current \(J\) of Unit 9.
Unit 4

Momentum, and the birth of operators

You carry over

\(p=mv\), and \(v=dx/dt\). Also integration by parts.

New ground
  • \(\langle x\rangle\) is an ensemble average, not a trajectory
  • Differentiating \(\langle x\rangle\) produces \(\langle p\rangle\) — and a differential operator
  • \(\hat p=-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial x\), and the recipe \(\hat Q=Q(x,\hat p)\)
DeriveThe momentum operator from \(d\langle x\rangle/dt\)5 marks
Step 1 · differentiate the average$$\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt}=\int x\,\frac{\partial|\Psi|^2}{\partial t}\,dx=\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\int x\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\Psi\right)dx$$ using Step 4 of the previous ladder.
Step 2 · integrate by parts onceThe boundary term dies at \(\pm\infty\), and \(\partial x/\partial x=1\): $$\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt}=-\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\int\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\Psi\right)dx$$
Step 3 · by parts again on the second term$$\int\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\Psi\,dx=-\int\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}dx \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt}=-\frac{i\hbar}{m}\int\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}dx$$
Step 4 · read off the operatorDefining \(\langle p\rangle=m\,d\langle x\rangle/dt\): $$\langle p\rangle=\int\Psi^*\left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)\Psi\,dx\;\Longrightarrow\;\boxed{\hat p=-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}},\quad \hat x=x$$
The recipe for any observable $$\langle Q(x,p)\rangle=\int\Psi^*\,Q\!\left(x,-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)\Psi\,dx,\qquad \hat T=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}$$

Every classical quantity is rewritten in terms of \(x\) and \(p\), then \(p\) is replaced by \(-i\hbar\partial_x\), and the result is sandwiched between \(\Psi^*\) and \(\Psi\). The whole Hamiltonian \(\hat H=\hat T+V\) is built this way.

Trap

\(\langle x\rangle\) is not "the average of repeated measurements on one particle" — the first measurement collapses the state and the rest would all return the same value. It is the average over an ensemble of identically prepared systems, each measured once. And \(d\langle x\rangle/dt=\langle p\rangle/m\) is a statement about averages, not a trajectory.

Unit 5

The uncertainty principle

You carry over

"\(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge h/4\pi\)", usually justified by a story about a microscope disturbing the electron.

New ground
  • The real origin: Fourier. Localised in \(x\) ⇒ broad in \(k\), always
  • \(\sigma\) means a standard deviation over an ensemble, not an instrument error
  • Equality holds — and only for a Gaussian

A wave with one exact wavelength stretches over all space; a wave confined to a small region needs a broad band of wavelengths to build it. That is a theorem about Fourier transforms, true of sound and water waves too. What makes it physics is de Broglie:

Heisenberg's relation $$p=\hbar k\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sigma_x\,\sigma_p\ \ge\ \frac{\hbar}{2}$$

\(\sigma_x=\sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle-\langle x\rangle^2}\) and \(\sigma_p=\sqrt{\langle p^2\rangle-\langle p\rangle^2}\), both computed from a single state \(\Psi\). A spread in wavelength is a spread in momentum, so a well-localised particle simply has no well-defined momentum to be disturbed.

Trap

This is not a statement that measuring position "kicks" the particle. It is a property of the state itself: prepare a thousand identical systems, measure position on half and momentum on the other half, and the two spreads obey the inequality. Write it with \(\sigma\), not \(\Delta\), if you want the marks.

CheckpointAn electron is confined to a region about \(1\,\)Å across. Estimate the minimum spread in its momentum, and the kinetic energy that implies.

\(\sigma_p\ge\dfrac{\hbar}{2\sigma_x}=\dfrac{1.055\times10^{-34}}{2\times10^{-10}}\approx5.3\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}\).

Then \(E\sim\dfrac{\sigma_p^2}{2m}=\dfrac{(5.3\times10^{-25})^2}{2\times9.11\times10^{-31}}\approx1.5\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\approx\mathbf{1\ eV}\).

That is the order of magnitude of atomic binding energies — and it is why an electron cannot simply sit still inside an atom. (Solving the box exactly gives \(E_1=37.6\) eV for \(a=1\) Å; the uncertainty estimate sets the scale, not the number.)
Module II · 13 hours · 20 marks · Griffiths §2.1, 2.2, 2.4 · Zettili §3.6.4, 4.4, 4.5

Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation

Six units and the biggest block of marks on the paper. One trick — separation of variables — turns the law of motion into an eigenvalue problem, and then you solve it four times: for a box, for free space, for a step and for a barrier.

Unit 6

Stationary states

You carry over

Separation of variables from your differential-equations course: if a PDE splits into a function of \(x\) times a function of \(t\), each side must equal a constant.

New ground
  • The separation constant is the energy
  • Four properties of separable solutions — the examinable list
  • The general solution is a sum, and only the sum can move
  • \(|c_n|^2\) as the probability of measuring \(E_n\)
DeriveSeparation of variables → the TISE5 marks · near-certain
Step 1 · the assumptionTake \(V=V(x)\) only, and look for solutions of the special form $$\Psi(x,t)=\psi(x)\,\phi(t)$$ (Not all solutions look like this — that is the point of Step 6.)
Step 2 · substitute$$i\hbar\,\psi\frac{d\phi}{dt}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}\phi+V\psi\phi$$
Step 3 · divide by \(\psi\phi\)$$i\hbar\frac{1}{\phi}\frac{d\phi}{dt}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{1}{\psi}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}+V(x)$$ The left side depends only on \(t\); the right only on \(x\). Two independent variables can only agree if both sides are the same constant, call it \(E\).
Step 4 · the time half$$\frac{d\phi}{dt}=-\frac{iE}{\hbar}\phi\;\Longrightarrow\;\phi(t)=e^{-iEt/\hbar}$$ A pure phase of constant modulus — this is why nothing observable moves.
Step 5 · the space half$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}+V(x)\psi=E\psi\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \hat H\psi=E\psi$$ An eigenvalue equation for the Hamiltonian. All the physics of a given potential is in its spectrum \(\{E_n\}\) and eigenfunctions \(\{\psi_n\}\).
Step 6 · rebuild the general solutionThe equation is linear, so superpose: $$\Psi(x,t)=\sum_{n}c_n\psi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar},\qquad c_n=\int\psi_n^*(x)\Psi(x,0)\,dx$$ Every solution can be written this way — that is what "complete" means for the set \(\{\psi_n\}\).

The four properties — write these as a numbered list

1 · They are stationary $$|\Psi(x,t)|^2=\psi^*e^{+iEt/\hbar}\psi e^{-iEt/\hbar}=|\psi(x)|^2$$

The phase cancels, so the probability density — and every expectation value of a time-independent operator — is frozen. In particular \(\langle x\rangle\) is constant and therefore \(\langle p\rangle=m\,d\langle x\rangle/dt=0\).

2 · They have a definite total energy $$\langle H\rangle=\int\psi^*\hat H\psi\,dx=E,\qquad \langle H^2\rangle=E^2\;\Longrightarrow\;\sigma_H^2=E^2-E^2=0$$

Zero variance means every measurement of the energy returns \(E\) — not an average, a certainty. This is what "the system has energy \(E\)" means quantum-mechanically.

3 · The general solution is a linear combination $$\Psi(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n\psi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$$

Each term carries its own rotating phase \(e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\). Different rates means the relative phases change, so a superposition does evolve — which is the only way anything ever happens.

4 · \(|c_n|^2\) is the probability of getting \(E_n\) $$\sum_n|c_n|^2=1,\qquad \langle H\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n$$

Both are constants of the motion, because the \(c_n\) never change — only their phases do. Measuring the energy of a superposition gives one of the \(E_n\), at random, with probability \(|c_n|^2\).

Six general theorems for one-dimensional potentials

  • \(E\) must be real for a normalisable solution.
  • \(\psi\) can always be taken real for a non-degenerate bound state (if \(\psi\) solves it, so does \(\psi^*\)).
  • If \(V(-x)=V(x)\), the eigenstates can be chosen with definite parity, even or odd.
  • \(E>V_{\min}\) always; otherwise \(\psi''\) and \(\psi\) share a sign everywhere and \(\psi\) blows up.
  • 1D bound states are non-degenerate.
  • Node theorem: the ground state has no interior nodes and the \(n\)-th state has \(n-1\) — a free sanity check on any sketch.

Checkpoint · say it out loudBoth \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\) are stationary states. Why is \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\psi_1+\psi_2)\) not?

Because the two terms carry different phase factors, \(e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}\) and \(e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\). In \(|\Psi|^2\) the cross term survives as \(\cos\!\big[(E_2-E_1)t/\hbar\big]\), so the density oscillates with period \(T=2\pi\hbar/(E_2-E_1)\). Only when a single term is present does the phase cancel against its own conjugate. Correspondingly \(\sigma_H\ne0\): the energy is no longer definite.
Unit 7

The infinite square well

You carry over

Standing waves on a string fixed at both ends: only whole numbers of half-wavelengths fit, \(L=n\lambda/2\).

New ground
  • The same boundary condition, now applied to \(\psi\), quantises the energy
  • \(E_n\propto n^2\) — an unevenly spaced ladder
  • Orthonormality and completeness, and how to get \(c_n\)
  • Zero-point energy: \(E_1\ne0\), and why it can't be
DeriveThe infinite square well, end to end8 marks · the safest question on the paper
Step 1 · set up$$V(x)=\begin{cases}0,&0\le x\le a\\ \infty,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$ Outside the well \(\psi=0\) (infinite potential ⇒ zero probability). Inside, the TISE is $$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}=E\psi\;\Longrightarrow\;\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}=-k^2\psi,\qquad k\equiv\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}$$
Step 2 · general solution$$\psi(x)=A\sin kx+B\cos kx$$ This is the classical SHM equation — you already know its solutions.
Step 3 · boundary conditions\(\psi\) must be continuous, so it must match the zero outside: \(\psi(0)=0\Rightarrow B=0\); \(\psi(a)=0\Rightarrow A\sin ka=0\). Since \(A=0\) would be no state at all, $$ka=n\pi,\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots$$ (\(n=0\) gives \(\psi\equiv0\); negative \(n\) only flips the sign, which is the same state.)
Step 4 · the energies fall out$$k_n=\frac{n\pi}{a}\;\Longrightarrow\;\boxed{E_n=\frac{\hbar^2k_n^2}{2m}=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}}$$ Energy is quantised purely because the wave has to fit — nothing was quantised by hand.
Step 5 · normalise$$\int_0^a|A|^2\sin^2\!\frac{n\pi x}{a}dx=|A|^2\frac{a}{2}=1\;\Longrightarrow\;A=\sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}$$ $$\boxed{\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin\!\frac{n\pi x}{a}}$$ (\(\int_0^a\sin^2=a/2\) — the average of \(\sin^2\) is \(\tfrac12\).)
Step 6 · the full time-dependent state$$\Psi(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n\sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin\!\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar},\qquad c_n=\sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\int_0^a\sin\!\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,\Psi(x,0)\,dx$$ using orthonormality \(\int_0^a\psi_m^*\psi_n dx=\delta_{mn}\).
Properties worth one line each

Alternately even and odd about the centre of the well · the \(n\)-th state has \(n-1\) interior nodes · they are mutually orthogonal · they are complete (this is just the Fourier sine series) · the levels get further apart as \(n\) grows, since \(E_n\propto n^2\).

Trap

\(n\) starts at 1, and \(E_1=\pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2\ne0\). A particle in a box can never be at rest: \(E=0\) would mean \(p=0\) exactly, violating \(\sigma_x\sigma_p\ge\hbar/2\) for a particle confined to width \(a\). That non-zero \(E_1\) is the zero-point energy.

CheckpointAn electron is in a 1 Å infinite well. Find \(E_1\) in eV, and say what \(E_3\) is without recomputing.

Use \(\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}=3.81\ \mathrm{eV\,\AA^2}\) — worth memorising.

\(E_1=\dfrac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m_ea^2}=\pi^2\times3.81/1^2=\mathbf{37.6\ eV}\).

\(E_3=9E_1=\mathbf{338\ eV}\), because \(E_n\propto n^2\). Note the scale: confining an electron to atomic dimensions costs tens of eV, which is exactly the scale of atomic binding energies.
Unit 8

The free particle: wave packets, phase and group velocity

You carry over

\(v=\nu\lambda=\omega/k\) for a travelling wave, and beats between two close frequencies.

New ground
  • The free particle has no normalisable stationary states
  • \(v_{\rm phase}=\tfrac12 v_{\rm classical}\) — an apparent disaster
  • Wave packets, and \(v_{\rm group}\) rescuing it exactly

With \(V=0\) the TISE gives \(\psi_k=Ae^{ikx}\), so

Free-particle solutions $$\Psi_k(x,t)=A\,e^{i\left(kx-\frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}t\right)},\qquad E=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m},\qquad p=\hbar k$$

\(k>0\) moves right, \(k<0\) moves left. But \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|A|^2dx=\infty\): a plane wave cannot be normalised, so it is not a physical state. It is a useful basis function, nothing more.

The velocity paradox $$v_{\rm phase}=\frac{\omega}{k}=\frac{\hbar k}{2m}=\sqrt{\frac{E}{2m}},\qquad v_{\rm classical}=\sqrt{\frac{2E}{m}}=2\,v_{\rm phase}$$

The wave crests travel at half the speed of the particle they are supposed to describe. The resolution is that no single \(k\) is a particle: build a packet.

DeriveGroup velocity, and why it is the particle's velocity5 marks
Step 1 · superpose a band of \(k\)$$\Psi(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(k)\,e^{i(kx-\omega(k)t)}dk,\qquad \omega(k)=\frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}$$ with \(\phi(k)\) the Fourier transform of \(\Psi(x,0)\). Now the state is normalisable.
Step 2 · narrow band around \(k_0\)If \(\phi(k)\) is sharply peaked at \(k_0\), expand $$\omega(k)\approx\omega_0+\omega_0'\,(k-k_0),\qquad \omega_0'=\left.\frac{d\omega}{dk}\right|_{k_0}$$
Step 3 · factor the integralSubstituting and letting \(s=k-k_0\): $$\Psi(x,t)\approx e^{i(k_0x-\omega_0 t)}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\phi(k_0+s)\,e^{is(x-\omega_0't)}ds$$ The first factor is a carrier travelling at \(\omega_0/k_0=v_{\rm phase}\); the integral is an envelope depending on \(x-\omega_0't\).
Step 4 · read the envelope speed$$v_{\rm group}=\left.\frac{d\omega}{dk}\right|_{k_0}=\frac{\hbar k_0}{m}=\frac{p_0}{m}=v_{\rm classical}=2v_{\rm phase}$$ The bump — where the particle actually is — moves at the classical speed. The ripples inside it move at half that, sliding backwards through the envelope.
Unit 9

Conservation of probability and the current density

You carry over

The continuity equation from fluids or from charge conservation: \(\partial\rho/\partial t+\nabla\!\cdot\!\vec J=0\) means "what leaves a region had to flow across its boundary".

New ground
  • Probability obeys exactly that equation
  • An explicit formula for \(J\), built out of \(\Psi\)
  • \(dP_{ab}/dt=J(a)-J(b)\) — the version you use in scattering
DeriveThe equation of continuity for probability5 marks
Step 1 · start where the last proof endedFrom Unit 3, Step 4: $$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi|^2=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\Psi\right)\right]$$
Step 2 · name the piecesPut \(\rho=|\Psi|^2\) and define $$J\equiv\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}-\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right)=\frac{\hbar}{m}\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right)$$ (the sign is chosen so that \(J>0\) means flow to the right).
Step 3 · the continuity equation$$\boxed{\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}=0}\qquad\text{3D: }\ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\vec\nabla\!\cdot\!\vec J=0$$
Step 4 · integrate over a region$$\frac{dP_{ab}}{dt}=J(a,t)-J(b,t)$$ The probability in \([a,b]\) changes only by flow in at \(a\) and out at \(b\). Probability is not created or destroyed, only transported — which is Unit 3's result stated locally.

Two quick consequences. For a real \(\psi\) (any bound state you can choose real), \(J=0\) everywhere — a bound state carries no net flow. For a plane wave \(Ae^{ikx}\), \(J=|A|^2\hbar k/m=|A|^2 v\): density times velocity, exactly as a flux should be. That second result is what you use to define \(R\) and \(T\) in Units 10 and 11.

Unit 10

The potential step

You carry over

Reflection and transmission of a wave at a boundary — a pulse on a light string meeting a heavy one is partly reflected.

New ground
  • Matching \(\psi\) and \(\psi'\) at the boundary — the whole method
  • \(R\) and \(T\) defined from currents, not amplitudes
  • Reflection even when \(E>V_0\); penetration even when \(E<V_0\)
Boundary conditions — memorise the reason, not just the rule

At any point where \(V\) is finite, \(\psi\) is continuous (otherwise \(\rho\) is ill-defined) and \(\psi'\) is continuous (integrate the TISE across an infinitesimal interval: a finite \(V\) contributes nothing). \(\psi'\) is allowed to jump only where \(V\) is infinite or a delta function; across \(\alpha\delta(x-x_0)\), \(\Delta\psi'=\frac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2}\psi(x_0)\).

DeriveStep with \(E>V_0\): reflection and transmission6 marks
Step 1 · write the two regions\(V=0\) for \(x<0\), \(V=V_0\) for \(x\ge0\); \(k_1=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\), \(k_2=\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}/\hbar\). $$\psi=\begin{cases}Ae^{ik_1x}+Be^{-ik_1x},&x<0\\ Ce^{ik_2x},&x\ge0\end{cases}$$ No \(e^{-ik_2x}\) on the right: nothing is coming back from \(+\infty\).
Step 2 · match at \(x=0\)$$A+B=C\quad\text{(continuity of }\psi)$$ $$k_1(A-B)=k_2C\quad\text{(continuity of }\psi')$$
Step 3 · solve the pair$$\frac{B}{A}=\frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2},\qquad \frac{C}{A}=\frac{2k_1}{k_1+k_2}$$
Step 4 · turn amplitudes into probabilities — carefully\(R\) and \(T\) are ratios of currents, and the transmitted wave travels at a different speed: $$R=\frac{|B|^2}{|A|^2}=\left(\frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2}\right)^2,\qquad T=\frac{k_2|C|^2}{k_1|A|^2}=\frac{4k_1k_2}{(k_1+k_2)^2}$$ Writing \(K=k_2/k_1=\sqrt{1-V_0/E}\): \(R=\left(\frac{1-K}{1+K}\right)^2\), \(T=\frac{4K}{(1+K)^2}\), and \(R+T=1\) exactly.
Step 5 · the case \(E<V_0\)Now \(k_2\to i\kappa\) with \(\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar\), and the right-hand solution is \(Ce^{-\kappa x}\) (the growing exponential is discarded). Then $$\frac{B}{A}=\frac{k_1-i\kappa}{k_1+i\kappa}\;\Longrightarrow\;R=\left|\frac{B}{A}\right|^2=1,\qquad T=0$$ Total reflection — yet \(\psi\ne0\) inside, decaying with penetration depth \(\delta=1/\kappa\). No contradiction: a real exponential carries \(J=0\).
Trap

\(T\ne|C/A|^2\). You must weight by the wave numbers, \(T=(k_2/k_1)|C/A|^2\), because transmission is a ratio of fluxes and the transmitted particle moves more slowly. Forget the \(k_2/k_1\) and \(R+T\) will not equal 1 — which is how the examiner spots it.

The classical comparison, worth stating explicitly. Classically a particle with \(E>V_0\) always continues (it just slows down) and one with \(E<V_0\) always turns back at exactly \(x=0\). Quantum mechanically the first is partly reflected, and the second enters the forbidden region before turning back. Both are pure wave behaviour, with no classical analogue for a particle.

Unit 11

The potential barrier and tunnelling

You carry over

The step, from the unit above. A barrier is two steps back to back — three regions instead of two, four matching conditions instead of two.

New ground
  • \(T\) for \(E>V_0\), with resonances where the barrier goes transparent
  • \(T\) for \(E<V_0\): tunnelling, forbidden classically
  • The exponential law \(T\sim e^{-2\kappa a}\), and what it explains
Above the barrier: \(E>V_0\) $$T=\left[1+\frac{V_0^2}{4E(E-V_0)}\sin^2(k_2a)\right]^{-1},\qquad k_2=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}}{\hbar}$$

Resonance: whenever \(k_2a=n\pi\) — that is, whenever the barrier width is a whole number of half-wavelengths inside — \(\sin^2=0\) and \(T=1\): the barrier becomes perfectly transparent. The reflections from the two faces cancel, exactly like a non-reflecting optical coating. This is the Ramsauer–Townsend effect in noble-gas scattering.

DeriveTunnelling: from the barrier formula in one substitution6 marks
Step 1 · the analytic continuationFor \(E<V_0\) the inside wave number becomes imaginary: put \(k_2\to i\kappa\), \(\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar\). Then \(\sin(k_2a)\to\sin(i\kappa a)=i\sinh(\kappa a)\), so \(\sin^2\to-\sinh^2\), and \(E-V_0\to-(V_0-E)\). Both sign flips cancel in the fraction.
Step 2 · the tunnelling formula$$\boxed{T=\left[1+\frac{V_0^2}{4E(V_0-E)}\sinh^2(\kappa a)\right]^{-1}}$$ Non-zero for every finite \(a\): the particle can be found on the far side of a barrier it could never climb.
Step 3 · thick or high barrierIf \(\kappa a\gg1\), \(\sinh(\kappa a)\approx\tfrac12 e^{\kappa a}\) and the 1 is negligible: $$T\approx16\,\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\,e^{-2\kappa a},\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{E}{V_0}$$ $$T\approx\frac{16E}{V_0}\!\left(1-\frac{E}{V_0}\right)\exp\!\left[-\frac{2a}{\hbar}\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}\right]$$
Step 4 · what the exponential means\(T\) depends exponentially on \(a\sqrt{V_0-E}\), so small changes in width or height change it by orders of magnitude. That extreme sensitivity is precisely what makes the scanning tunnelling microscope able to resolve single atoms, and it is why \(\alpha\)-decay half-lives span from microseconds to \(10^{17}\) years for a barrier that changes by only a few MeV (the Gamow theory). Also: the tunnel diode, and the \(23\,\)GHz inversion of the ammonia molecule.

CheckpointAn electron of 5 eV meets a 10 eV barrier 1 Å wide. Find \(T\). Then say what happens to \(T\) if the barrier is 5 Å wide.

\(\kappa=\dfrac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}\). With \(\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}=3.81\ \mathrm{eV\,\AA^2}\): \(\kappa=\sqrt{5/3.81}=1.15\ \mathrm{\AA^{-1}}\), so \(\kappa a=1.15\).

\(\varepsilon=0.5\), so \(4\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)=1\) and \(T=\left[1+\sinh^2(1.15)\right]^{-1}=\left[1+2.02\right]^{-1}\approx\mathbf{0.33}\).

At \(a=5\,\)Å, \(\kappa a=5.75\), \(\sinh^2\approx2.4\times10^4\), so \(T\approx\mathbf{4\times10^{-5}}\). Five times the width, roughly eight thousand times less transmission — that is the exponential at work.

Moves you are expected to make without comment

Integration by parts, boundary term dropped

\(\int u\,dv=[uv]-\int v\,du\), and \([uv]_{-\infty}^{\infty}=0\) because a normalisable \(\Psi\) and \(\partial_x\Psi\) vanish at infinity. Used twice in the momentum derivation alone.

Take the complex conjugate of the equation

Conjugating the Schrödinger equation flips \(i\to-i\) and leaves \(V\) alone because \(V\) is real. Say that out loud — it is a mark.

Euler, both ways

\(e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\); \(|e^{i\theta}|=1\); \(\sin\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}\). Converting between \(A\sin kx+B\cos kx\) and \(Ce^{ikx}+De^{-ikx}\) is free.

Imaginary argument identities

\(\sin(i\theta)=i\sinh\theta\), \(\cos(i\theta)=\cosh\theta\). This one substitution converts the barrier formula into the tunnelling formula.

\(|z|^2=zz^*\), never \(z^2\)

For \(z=\frac{k_1-i\kappa}{k_1+i\kappa}\), \(|z|^2=\frac{k_1^2+\kappa^2}{k_1^2+\kappa^2}=1\). Squaring instead of conjugating is the classic way to lose the \(R=1\) result.

Separation of variables

Assume \(F(x)G(t)\), divide by \(FG\), argue each side is constant. The constant always turns out to be the conserved quantity — here, the energy.

Fourier pair, in the convention this course uses

Position ↔ momentum $$\Psi(x,0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(k)e^{ikx}dk\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \phi(k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi(x,0)e^{-ikx}dx$$

\(|\phi(k)|^2dk\) is the probability of finding momentum in \([\hbar k,\hbar(k+dk)]\). Narrow in \(x\) ⇒ wide in \(k\): the uncertainty principle is a property of this pair, not of any apparatus.

Delta function, for the odd question that needs it

  • \(\int f(x)\delta(x-a)dx=f(a)\)
  • \(\delta(cx)=\frac{1}{|c|}\delta(x)\)
  • Across \(V=\alpha\delta(x-x_0)\): \(\psi\) stays continuous but \(\Delta\psi'=\frac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2}\psi(x_0)\)

Ten traps that eat marks

7:30 – 10:00
  1. \(|\Psi|^2\) is a probability density, not a probability — always integrate over an interval.
  2. \(\langle x\rangle\) is an ensemble average over identically prepared systems, not repeated measurements on one particle, and not a trajectory.
  3. Normalisation fixes \(|A|\) only; the overall phase \(e^{i\theta}\) is unobservable and never determined.
  4. The proof that normalisation survives needs \(V\) real — say so when you use it.
  5. A stationary state is not a particle at rest: \(\langle p\rangle=0\) but \(\langle p^2\rangle\ne0\).
  6. \(|c_n|^2\) is the probability of measuring the energy \(E_n\), not of finding the particle anywhere.
  7. In the infinite well \(n\) starts at 1; \(n=0\) gives \(\psi\equiv0\), and \(E_1\ne0\) is the zero-point energy.
  8. \(T=(k_2/k_1)|C/A|^2\). Drop the ratio of wave numbers and \(R+T\ne1\).
  9. For \(E<V_0\) at a step, \(R=1\) and \(\psi\ne0\) inside — no contradiction, because a real exponential carries zero current.
  10. Phase velocity is not the particle's velocity; group velocity is, and \(v_{\rm gr}=2v_{\rm ph}\) for a free particle.
